# PlotMate crop notes

Growing and kitchen notes for fruit, vegetables and herbs commonly grown on UK allotments. Measurements are given in imperial first, with metric in brackets. The advice is written for ordinary garden soil and raised beds alike.

## Legumes

### Beans for drying

**Sow:** Start under cover from mid-April through May, pushing one bean 2in (5cm) deep into a small pot or deep module of damp peat-free potting mix, and keep it warmer than 54°F (12°C) on a bright indoor sill, under a propagator lid, or in a glasshouse. Direct sowing waits until frost has finished and the ground itself is at least 54°F (12°C) – typically around the middle of May in southern districts, and the start of June in the north. Set seed 2in (5cm) deep, one or two at the foot of each cane (thin to a single seedling), or 4in (10cm) apart in rows 18in (45cm) apart for dwarf types, with a few spare seeds at the row ends to fill blanks.

**Plant out:** After the last frost, usually late May or early June, once indoor-raised plants are 4–6in (10–15cm) tall. Harden off first and have supports already in place. Tie climbing plants loosely to their canes.

**Spacing:** One climbing plant per cane. Dwarf plants 1ft (30cm) apart when transplanted (or 4in (10cm) in the seed row, with 18in (45cm) between rows). Cane A-frames use 8ft (2.5m) poles, 6–12in (15–30cm) apart, with 18in–2ft (45–60cm) between rows of supports.

**Care:** Choose a warm, open, sunny site with light, free-draining soil. Work in at least two buckets of garden compost or well-rotted manure per square yard (square metre) a few weeks ahead, or, for no-dig, mulch and sow or plant into the mulch. Set supports before sowing. Water regularly in dry weather, especially once plants flower and pods swell; use stored rainwater if you can, and water at the cooler ends of hot days. A thick compost or manure mulch helps hold moisture and slows weeds. When climbers reach the top of the canes, pinch out the growing tips so side-shoots form lower down and the plants do not become top-heavy. Tie in any stems loosened by wind.

**Harvest window:** From July, according to sowing date, until before the first frost (calendar July–October). Pods are ready when they are dry, shrivelled and brittle. Avoid picking just after rain. If frost threatens before pods have fully ripened, shell the nearly mature beans and freeze them.

**Storing:** Shell the pods, spread the beans on a tray in a warm place until they are hard and fully dry, then keep them in airtight jars for soups, stews and casseroles. After cropping, cut the tops for the compost heap but leave the nitrogen-fixing roots in the soil.

**Common problems:** Bean seed fly, black bean aphid (blackfly), rust on runner and French beans, slugs and snails.

**Varieties:**

- **'Borlotto Lingua di Fuoco'** (recommended)
- **'Czar'** (recommended)
- **'Yin Yang'** (recommended)

### Broad beans

**Sow:** Indoors from February, one large seed 2in (5cm) deep in a small pot or module of peat-free compost, scar facing down; useful where soil is wet (seeds can rot) or mice are a problem. Outdoors in March, April and early May for a summer crop. In mild or very sheltered, well-drained sites, hardy varieties can also be sown in October–November or February, with cloches or fleece in cold spells.

**Plant out:** Early to mid-spring for indoor-raised or bought plants, after hardening off.

**Spacing:** Plants 6–9in (15–23cm) apart, either in single rows 18in (45cm) apart or in double rows 9in (23cm) apart with 2ft (60cm) between each pair of rows. Dwarf types can go in a container at least 15in (40cm) wide, 6in (15cm) apart, in a sunny, sheltered position.

**Care:** A warm, sunny, sheltered, well-drained site. Fork in at least two buckets of well-rotted manure or garden compost per square yard (square metre) a few weeks ahead, or mulch and sow or plant into the mulch for no-dig. In the open ground, plants seldom need routine watering except on light soils, but a thorough soak at first flower and again a fortnight later improves the crop. Pots need frequent watering, and a high-potassium liquid feed such as tomato fertiliser every two weeks once flowering starts. Mulch after planting. Hoe between rows while plants are young. Most varieties need support: stout stakes at the corners and every 4ft (1.2m) along the row, with string run around at 1ft (30cm) heights. Dwarf types often hold each other up in double rows.

**Harvest window:** May–August, depending on sowing date and variety. For shelling, take pods that are well filled but still fairly small; the hilum (the mark where the bean joins the pod) should still look pale, not darkened. Very young pods about 2in (5cm) long can be cooked whole. Lower pods ripen first; pick often, with scissors or secateurs, to keep plants cropping. Young shoot tips can be cooked like spinach.

**Storing:** Not a long-keeping crop. After harvest, cut the tops for compost and leave the nitrogen-fixing roots in place, or dig the whole plants in.

**Common problems:** Chocolate spot, rust, black bean aphid (blackfly), broad bean seed beetle, mice and voles, pea and bean weevils.

**In the kitchen:** Broad, or fava, beans form thick, leathery, inedible pods 6–10 inches (15–25 cm) long and about ¾–1 inch (2–3 cm) across, holding two to eight flat green beans, each about ⅕–⅜ inch (5–10 mm), in a cottony lining. Young beans can be eaten fresh and taste sweet with a slight bitterness; mature ones turn starchy and need cooking, and many people also slip off the outer skin. They are among the oldest domesticated pulses, with remains in Israel dated to around 6800–6500 BCE, later spreading from the Middle East into the Mediterranean.

**Varieties:**

- **'Aquadulce Claudia' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Dreadnought' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Witkiem' - Manita AGM** (recommended)
- **'The Sutton' AGM** (recommended)

### French beans

**Sow:** Indoors from late April, one seed 2in (5cm) deep in a small pot or deep module of moist peat-free compost, on a warm sunny windowsill or in a propagator, for earlier and more reliable germination and to keep seedlings away from slugs. Outdoors after the last frost once the soil is warming, usually late May or early June. An indoor batch followed by a later outdoor sowing stretches the season. Calendar: sow April–July.

**Plant out:** After the last frost, usually late May or early June. Harden off, water before and after planting, and have supports ready for climbing types.

**Spacing:** 6in (15cm) between plants. Plant dwarf types in blocks or double rows so they prop each other up; a few twiggy sticks help keep pods off the soil. One climber at the base of each cane, shoots tied in loosely. Containers: 12–18in (30–45cm) wide for dwarf plants; 30in (75cm) wide and 18in (45cm) deep for climbers. Climbing supports: 8ft (2.5m) canes 6in (15cm) apart in the row, rows at least 18in (45cm) apart, tied as an A-frame or X-frame.

**Care:** Warm, sunny, well-drained ground with at least two buckets of compost or well-rotted manure per square yard (square metre) worked in ahead of time, or a no-dig mulch. Water in dry weather, especially once flowering and pod set begin; pots dry out quickly. Mulch after planting. Plants in the open ground do not usually need feed; in containers, give a high-potassium liquid feed such as tomato fertiliser every two weeks from first flower. When climbers reach the top of the support, pinch out the tips.

**Harvest window:** July–October. Pick pods at 6–8in (15–20cm) long, while they snap cleanly and before the beans show through the pod. Regular picking keeps plants producing; dwarf types crop for several weeks if sown in succession, climbers for longer.

**Storing:** Flowers are self-pollinating, so seed saved from leftover mature, dried pods can be stored cool and dry in a labelled paper bag for sowing the next spring. After cropping, cut the tops for compost and leave the nitrogen-fixing roots in the soil.

**Common problems:** Bean seed fly, black bean aphid (blackfly), rust on runner and French beans, slugs and snails.

**In the kitchen:** French beans are slim, tender pods, typically 3–4 inches (7–10 cm) long, straight or slightly curved, in green, purple or cream, with tiny seeds and a crisp, grassy flavour. They cook in minutes — sauté, blanch, roast or stir-fry them whole or sliced. Like other common beans they come from Central and South America, and reached Europe with Spanish and Portuguese travellers in the 16th century.

**Varieties:**

- **'Algarve' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Cobra' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Golden Gate' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Boston' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Purple Teepee'** (recommended)
- **'Sprite' AGM** (recommended)
- **Blue Lake bean** — Blue Lake is a slender, stringless green bean, typically 5½–7 inches (14–18 cm) long, plump and crisp when raw, with a fresh green taste that mellows to something milder and grassy once cooked. Steam, sauté, roast or stir-fry the pods whole or sliced, or toss them into salads. The type descends from Andean beans that Native American growers later planted in the ‘three sisters’ system with maize and squash.
- **Dragon Tongue bean** — Dragon Tongue is an 18th-century Dutch heirloom: a broad, flat, stringless pod 5–8 inches (13–20 cm) long, cream with bright purple streaks when ready, holding four to six firm, slightly starchy white seeds. Eat it raw to keep the colour — cooking fades the purple — or cook it like any other fresh bean; the mild, earthy aroma deepens in the pan. It likes a temperate, sunny season and will not stand frost.
- **Romano (flat) bean** — Romano, or Italian flat beans, are broad, slightly wavy green pods, typically 4–6 inches (10–15 cm) long (sometimes longer at the height of the season), with a fresh, sweet, grassy flavour. The whole pod is eaten, raw or cooked, and can replace ordinary green beans in almost any dish. They descend from Mesoamerican and South American Phaseolus types that Spanish and Portuguese explorers carried to Europe in the 15th and 16th centuries.
- **Purple bean** — Purple beans are slim, stringless cylindrical pods, typically 4–7 inches (10–17 cm) long, in shades from violet to royal purple, with a mild, slightly sweet, grassy taste. Sliced raw they show a bright green interior against the purple skin, which is why they are often used fresh; the colour usually dulls with heat. Like other common beans they originated in the Andes and were domesticated separately in South America and Mesoamerica.

### Peas

**Sow:** Under cover in February or March (and optionally in autumn, to overwinter in a glasshouse or cold frame for an early-spring move), 2in (5cm) deep: two or three seeds to a pot, or a single seed in each module or cardboard tube; or space them 3in (7.5cm) apart in a double line along compost-filled guttering so the row can be slid out later. Starting indoors helps if the soil is cold and wet or mice are present. In the open, wait until the ground is at least 50°F (10°C), from March (calendar February–June). Young pea shoots can be started under cover in any month if you want a fast picking of tender tips.

**Plant out:** From March onwards once indoor-raised plants are about 8in (20cm) tall, after hardening off. Water well before and after moving them, and disturb the roots as little as possible. Gutter-sown rows can be slid into a matching shallow trench.

**Spacing:** About 3in (7.5cm) between plants. Shorter varieties in a triple row; taller cultivars in one line, or in a pair of lines 1ft (30cm) apart. Leave a gap between successive sets of rows about equal to the plants' eventual height, for air movement, and put supports in at planting. Dwarf or faster mangetout types can be grown in a large container, though they crop better in the open ground.

**Care:** Warm, sunny, sheltered, well-drained ground with at least two buckets of compost or well-rotted manure per square yard (square metre) forked in a few weeks ahead, or a no-dig mulch. Once established, water mainly in very dry spells, then give a good soak at first flower and again a fortnight later; check moisture at root depth while plants are cropping, and water the soil rather than the leaves. Pots need regular watering and a high-potassium organic liquid feed such as tomato fertiliser every two weeks from first flower. Mulch after planting. Routine fertiliser is not needed in reasonably fertile ground.

**Harvest window:** June–October, according to type and variety. Pick often or plants stop flowering. Shelling peas are ready when the pods are well filled; mangetout and sugarsnaps at about 3in (7.5cm) long, just as the peas begin to form. Pods swell from the bottom of the plant upwards. Young shoot tips can be eaten, but do not take too many from plants grown for pods.

**Storing:** Eat soon after picking for the best sweetness, or freeze any surplus rather than leaving pods on the plant. After cropping, cut the tops for compost and leave the nitrogen-fixing roots in the soil.

**Common problems:** Plant viruses, powdery mildew, mice and voles, pea and bean weevils, pea moth, pigeons, slugs and snails, thrips.

**Varieties:**

- **'Hurst Green Shaft' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Jaguar' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Kelvedon Wonder' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Delikata' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Vivado' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Terrain' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Delikett' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Sugar Ann' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Cascadia' AGM** (recommended)
- **Garden (English) pea** — Garden, or English, peas are the familiar shelled pea: fibrous pods that are snapped and strung to release five to ten sweet, grassy seeds. The pods themselves are too tough to eat. Use the peas raw as a snack or quickly cooked in any spring dish. Pisum sativum is one of the oldest crops, with wild ancestors around the Mediterranean and Middle East, and carbonised remains have been found at early archaeological sites.
- **Sugar snap pea** — Sugar snaps are edible-podded peas with thick, plump walls and a sweet, snappy bite, eaten pod and all. Breeders crossed a Chinese snow pea with an unusual mutant of the garden pea; the resulting group later took an All-America Selections gold medal. The source page does not give kitchen detail beyond that, but the whole pod is the point of the vegetable.
- **Speckled snow pea** — Speckled snow peas are flat, sometimes twisted pods, about 2–4 inches (5–10 cm) long, uniquely mottled in maroon and gold, with four to seven small seeds showing as bumps under the skin. The flavour is fresh, sweet and green, and they cook faster than many peas — stir-fry, sauté or blanch them, or eat them raw. Calvin Lamborn, who also raised the sugar snap, bred this type by conventional crossing on his farm in Twin Falls, Idaho.

### Runner beans

**Sow:** Under cover from mid-April to May, one seed 2in (5cm) deep in a small pot or deep module of damp peat-free potting mix, kept above 54°F (12°C). This brings earlier, surer germination and keeps seedlings away from slugs. In the open, wait until frost has finished and the ground is at least 54°F (12°C) – typically around the middle of May in southern districts, and the start of June in the north – 2in (5cm) deep, two seeds at the foot of each cane, later thinned to one. Calendar: sow April–July.

**Plant out:** Late May or early June, once frost risk has passed. Harden off, set supports first, plant one climber at the base of each cane, water well and tie stems in loosely.

**Spacing:** One climbing plant per cane. Dwarf types 1ft (30cm) apart; planting through a plastic-free weed-suppressing membrane keeps pods off the soil and cuts weeding. Traditional A-frame: 8ft (2.5m) canes, 6–12in (15–30cm) apart in the row, 18in–2ft (45–60cm) between the two rows, tied to a ridge cane; leave at least 5ft (1.5m) between successive double rows. An X-frame is easier to pick but less stable. Containers: 30in (75cm) wide and 18in (45cm) deep for climbers, 12–18in (30–45cm) wide for dwarfs.

**Care:** Sunny and sheltered, with at least two buckets of compost or well-rotted manure per square yard (square metre) forked in ahead of time, or a no-dig mulch. These are thirsty plants and crop best if the roots stay moist, especially once they flower and set pods; containers need frequent, generous watering. Mulch after planting. Open-ground plants rarely need feed; in pots, use a high-potassium organic liquid feed such as tomato fertiliser every two weeks from first flower. Guard against slugs. When climbers reach the top of the support, pinch out the tips and tie in loose stems.

**Harvest window:** July–October, typically from mid-summer and for eight weeks or more if you keep picking. Take pods young, tender and stringless, at 8–10in (20–25cm) long, when they snap easily and the beans inside are still small and pale. Pick every two or three days; if pods mature, flowering stops.

**Storing:** Best used young and fresh, when they are at their most tender, rather than stored. After cropping, cut the tops for compost and leave the nitrogen-fixing roots in the soil.

**Common problems:** Failure to set pods, black bean aphid (blackfly), glasshouse red spider mite, green shieldbugs, rust on runner and French beans, slugs and snails.

**In the kitchen:** Runner beans are long, flat, bright-green pods that can reach 10 inches (25 cm) but are at their best at 6–8 inches (15–20 cm), while the interior is still crisp and the seeds small; older pods turn stringy. Unless very young, they are cooked: stringed, sliced, and boiled or steamed. The plant comes from the highlands of Central America, reached Spain, and is thought to have been introduced to England in the 17th century by the plant collector John Tradescant the Younger.

**Varieties:**

- **'White Lady' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Stardust' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Liberty' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Enorma' – Elite AGM** (recommended)
- **'Firelight'** (recommended)
- **'Moonlight' AGM** (recommended)

### Soya beans

**Sow:** Indoors in April and May is the more reliable option: 2in (5cm) deep in small pots or modules of moist peat-free seed compost, germinated at 64–68°F (18–20°C) in a propagator or under a clear bag, then grown on in warmth and good light; pot on when roots show through the drainage holes. Outdoors in May and June once the soil is at least 60°F (15°C), 2in (5cm) deep and 6in (15cm) apart, rows 18in (45cm) apart, then covered with cloches or plastic-free fleece. Protect seedlings from slugs and snails.

**Plant out:** Late May or early June, when temperatures are around 60°F (15°C). Harden off, water before and after planting, then cover with cloches or fleece while plants settle.

**Spacing:** 6in (15cm) between plants, 18in (45cm) between rows.

**Care:** Warm, open, sunny site, ideally light and free-draining, with at least two buckets of compost or well-rotted manure per square yard (square metre) forked in ahead of time, or a no-dig mulch. Water regularly in dry spells, especially once flowering and pod set begin, and keep weeds down. Mulch deeply after planting. Most UK varieties reach about 2ft (60cm) and stand on their own; in wind, or with taller types, use end canes and a loop of string.

**Harvest window:** August–October. Pick as green edamame in summer while pods are still green and well filled, or as dried beans from late September when leaves drop and brown pods hang on the stems. Both immature and ripe beans must be boiled thoroughly (edamame for at least ten minutes) to destroy natural toxins. Ripe pods stay weatherproof, so you can pick as needed through autumn.

**Storing:** Shell dry pods and either cook at once or spread the beans on an indoor tray to finish drying, then keep in an airtight container for winter. After cropping, cut the tops for compost and leave the nitrogen-fixing roots in the soil.

**Common problems:** Downy mildew, plant viruses, powdery mildew, slugs and snails.

**Varieties:**

- **'Elena'** (recommended)
- **'Envy'** (recommended)
- **'Black Jet'** (recommended)

## Brassicas

### Broccoli

**Sow:** Under cover from February, two seeds ¾in (2cm) deep in each module of peat-free seed mix, thinned to one; they usually come up within a fortnight without extra heat. Move plants out when they are 4–6in (10–15cm) tall and well rooted. Outdoors from April, in a ¾in (2cm) drill: 3in (7.5cm) apart in a nursery row, or three seeds every 24–31in (60–80cm) in the final bed, later thinned to one. Sow short batches several weeks apart. The same seed can be sprouted as tiny salad shoots on a bright indoor sill in any month. Calendar: sow February–July.

**Plant out:** From mid-spring to late summer (April–August) once plants are 4–6in (10–15cm) tall. Harden off indoor seedlings, water before and after the move, and set them deeper than before so the lowest leaves sit at soil level.

**Spacing:** 24–31in (60–80cm) between plants and a similar gap between rows, according to the eventual size of the variety.

**Care:** Fertile, well-drained ground in sun or very light shade, pH 6–7; avoid winter waterlogging for overwintering types. Use a no-dig mulch if that is your method, and rotate with other brassicas. Water young plants in dry spells, then only in long droughts. Mulch thickly. On poor soil, an organic high-nitrogen feed when plants are about 8in (20cm) tall can help. Weed while they are young. Plants often reach 2–3ft (60–90cm) and usually need a cane in exposed sites; wind-rock loosens roots. Keep a fine-mesh net or insect-proof mesh, or biodegradable fleece, over the crop at all times against pigeons and brassica pests, and guard seedlings from slugs.

**Harvest window:** With successional sowings, from June into the following March (calendar also lists February, March and June–November). Cut the whole shoot at the base, taking about 4in (10cm) of stem and the small leaves, all of which are edible.

**Storing:** A fresh, successional crop; no long-term store is described.

**Common problems:** Club root, cabbage caterpillars, cabbage root fly, cabbage whitefly, flea beetles, mealy cabbage aphid, white blister, pigeons, slugs and snails.

**In the kitchen:** Broccoli is a tight head of unopened green flower buds, typically 4–8 inches (10–20 cm) across, on a thick stalk among leathery dark-green leaves; some types flush purple. Buds, stalks and foliage can all go in the pot, and the vegetable is just as good raw as it is steamed, roasted, fried or sautéed. It was selected over many generations from a wild Mediterranean and Asia Minor cabbage relative, bred for a better flavour, size and texture.

**Varieties:**

- **'Cardinal' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Claret' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Mendocino' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Red Admiral'** (recommended)
- **'Red Fire' AGM** (recommended)
- **Purple sprouting broccoli** — Purple sprouting broccoli makes a small deep-purple central head and a dozen or so similar spears on thinner stems, usually cut at about 5 inches (13 cm) to keep the plant shooting. It is nutty and peppery, more tender than heading broccoli, and cooks quickly over high heat, though the purple colour fades in the pan. First grown in southern Italy, ‘Sprouting Purple broccoli’ was listed by Vilmorin-Andrieux in 1885, which also introduced it to American gardeners.
- **Rapini (broccoli rabe)** — Rapini, or broccoli rabe, is a young leafy brassica 6–10 inches (15–25 cm) long, with slender stalks, large leaves and only small clusters of buds rather than a tight head; a few yellow flowers may already be open. Stems, leaves and buds are all eaten, and the flavour is sharp, bitter and nutty, best tamed by steaming, roasting, boiling or a good sauté. Its origin is disputed — southern Italy or China — but it was selected from a wild mustard over a long period.

### Brussels sprouts

**Sow:** Start early, under cover or outdoors under cloches, because the crop needs a long season. Indoors, two seeds ¾in (2cm) deep in each peat-free module, thinned to one, grown in good light and planted from April once at least 4in (10cm) tall and well rooted; use cloches or biodegradable fleece if it is still cold. Outdoors, thinly ¾in (2cm) deep in a nursery row 6in (15cm) apart, thinned to 3in (7.5cm), for an early-summer move; or a few seeds every 2ft (60cm) in the final bed, thinned to the strongest, with at least 2ft (60cm) between rows. Calendar: sow February–May.

**Plant out:** Late spring or early summer (April–June) at 4–6in (10–15cm) tall. Harden off indoor plants, water the day before, set them deeper so the lowest leaves sit at soil level, firm and water.

**Spacing:** 2ft (60cm) between plants, and at least 2ft (60cm) between rows.

**Care:** Sunny, fertile, firm, well-drained ground. If the soil is very acid, lime the winter before to lift pH to at least 6.5 and discourage club root. Rotate with other brassicas, or plant into a no-dig mulch. Water young plants so the soil never dries, then only in drought or on very free-draining ground. Mulch to hold moisture. On poor soil, or if growth is slow, a nitrogen-rich feed in June or July can help. Weed while plants are young. In late summer, mound soil around the stems for autumn gales; these top-heavy plants loosen at the root if they rock. Strip fading leaves to improve airflow. Keep fine-mesh netting over the crop against cabbage butterflies and pigeons, and protect youngsters from slugs.

**Harvest window:** Early varieties from autumn; the main season is through winter (calendar September–February). Flavour is sweeter after frost. Snap a few tight, bright-green buttons from each plant along the row with a downward tug, and keep picking.

**Storing:** If you cut a whole stem, stand it in a jar of water for a short time, or hang it somewhere cool and safe from frost, such as a garage or shed, and pick buttons as needed.

**Common problems:** Club root, cabbage caterpillars, cabbage root fly, cabbage whitefly, flea beetles, mealy cabbage aphid, white blister, pigeons, slugs and snails.

**In the kitchen:** Brussels sprouts are tight, oval buds, about ½–1½ inches (1.5–4 cm) across, that cluster up a thick, upright stalk between the leaves. Washed and trimmed of the outer leaves, they have an earthy, nutty, slightly bitter brassica flavour, and can be halved and roasted, shredded raw, or cooked in almost any savoury way. They descend from wild Mediterranean cabbage-like plants that were later selected and grown further north in Europe.

**Varieties:**

- **'Igor' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Cascade' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Crispus' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Maximus' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Red Bull'** (recommended)
- **Purple Brussels sprouts** — Purple Brussels sprouts are a little looser than the green ones, typically ¾–2 inches (2–5 cm) across, with layered leaves in violet shades and a subtly sweet, earthy, nutty taste. Slice them raw into slaws and winter salads, or cook them as you would green sprouts. They share the same wild-cabbage ancestry as the familiar green type.

### Cabbages

**Sow:** Indoors, two seeds ½in (1cm) deep in each peat-free module, thinned to one; after about five weeks, at 4–6in (10–15cm) tall and well rooted, they can go out. Outdoors, traditionally in a nursery bed, or in the final row with later thinning. Timing: February–April for summer types (cloches or biodegradable fleece on early sowings); April–June for autumn and winter types; July for spring types. Sow in a ¾in (2cm) drill: 4in (10cm) apart in a seedbed, or three seeds every 12–18in (30–45cm) in the final bed, thinned to one. Calendar: sow February–July. They can also be grown as quick windowsill microgreens.

**Plant out:** When plants are 4–6in (10–15cm) tall with five or six true leaves (March–October on the calendar, according to type). Harden off indoor seedlings, water the day before, set them deeper so the lowest leaves sit at soil level, firm and water generously.

**Spacing:** As a rule, compact types 1ft (30cm) apart and larger ones up to 18in (45cm); check the packet. Spring cabbages can be set 4in (10cm) apart at first, then thinned in late February or March to 1ft (30cm), using the extras as spring greens. In pots: one plant in a 12in (30cm) pot, or up to three in a 20in (50cm) pot, planted deeply; they are not suited to growing bags. Give plenty of light and air; do not crowd them.

**Care:** Sunny, fertile, free-draining ground where brassicas have not grown for a couple of years; avoid winter waterlogging for overwintering types. Lime very acid soil the winter before to lift pH above 6 and discourage club root, or plant into a no-dig mulch. Water young plants often (their roots are small); once established, a thorough soak every 10 days in dry spells is usually enough, with extra water as heads form to increase size. Mulch thickly. On poor soil, a nitrogen-rich feed after plants have settled, but before they heart up, encourages leaf; give spring types this boost in early spring. Hoe while plants are young. Keep insect-proof mesh over the crop against caterpillars, root fly, whitefly and pigeons, and watch for slugs. Stand pots in sun and never let them dry in summer.

**Harvest window:** Year-round if you grow spring, summer, autumn and winter types (calendar January–December). Cut through the stem base once heads reach a useful size; taking every other plant first lets the rest bulk up. On spring and summer types, a ½in (1cm) deep cross cut in the stump can produce a smaller second head.

**Storing:** Certain winter types will hold for some months if kept cool, dry and safe from frost.

### Calabrese

**Sow:** Usually March–June: under cover from early to mid-spring, outdoors from mid-spring to early summer, in short batches a few weeks apart. Mid-autumn module sowings can be overwintered in an unheated glasshouse for a spring crop. Indoors, two seeds ¾in (2cm) deep per peat-free module, thinned to one, moved out at 4–6in (10–15cm) tall. Outdoors, a ¾in (2cm) drill: thinly, ideally 3in (7.5cm) apart, in a nursery row; or three seeds every 1ft (30cm) in the final bed (up to 18in / 45cm for larger types – check the packet), thinned to one, with at least 18in (45cm) between rows. Cover with insect-proof mesh.

**Plant out:** At 4–6in (10–15cm) tall, ideally before hot weather (April–June). Harden off, avoid root disturbance, water before and after, and plant deeper so the lowest leaves sit at soil level.

**Spacing:** 1ft (30cm) apart, or 18in (45cm) for larger varieties, with 18in (45cm) between rows.

On poor soil, or if growth is slow, an organic high-nitrogen feed when plants are about 8in (20cm) tall can help. As the main head forms, mound soil around the stem, and cane tall or exposed plants, so wind does not loosen the roots. Keep insect-proof mesh over the crop against pigeons and brassica insects, and weed regularly.

**Harvest window:** July–October. Cut the main head at 4–6in (10–15cm) across, while it is still tight in bud and before flowers open.

**Storing:** This is a late-summer and autumn fresh harvest; no long store is given.

**Common problems:** Club root, cabbage caterpillars, cabbage root fly, cabbage whitefly, flea beetles, mealy cabbage aphid, white blister, pigeons, slugs and snails.

### Cauliflower

**Sow:** Usually started under cover; outdoor sowing is possible from mid- to late spring. Sow small batches about three weeks apart, because a single sowing often matures all at once and the heads do not keep well. Indoors, sow about ¾in (1.5cm) deep in peat-free seed mix at 54–72°F (12–22°C), and transplant after about six weeks once roots fill the module. Outdoors, a ¾in (1.5cm) drill: 4–6in (10–15cm) apart in a nursery row, with at least 6in (15cm) between seedbed rows, for a move at about 4in (10cm) tall (around six weeks). Calendar: sow January–June.

**Plant out:** At 4–6in (10–15cm) tall, about six weeks old (March–August). Harden off indoor plants, water before lifting, set them deeper so the lowest leaves sit at soil level, firm and water. Cover against slugs, brassica insects and birds with insect-proof mesh.

**Spacing:** Summer and autumn types: 18–24in (45–60cm) between plants, 24in (60cm) between rows. Winter and spring types: 28in (70cm) each way. Mini caulis: 6in (15cm) each way, planted close to keep heads small. Up to four mini plants will fit a 16in (40cm) pot of peat-free compost, planted deeply; stand it in a bright, sheltered place and never let the compost dry.

**Care:** Sunny site with deep, fertile soil that stays moist but drains; pH around 6.5. Do not follow other brassicas for at least two years, or plant into a no-dig mulch. Keep the soil damp at all times – dryness can stall growth and spoil the curd. Water seedlings and young plants until they root deeply. Mulch thickly. Feed is not usually needed; on poor soil, an organic nitrogen-rich feed before heads start to form can help. Weed while plants are young. In spring, cloches or biodegradable fleece protect young plants from frost, which can cause bolting. In winter, fleece the curd and/or fold leaves over it and tie them, to shield it from hard frost.

**Harvest window:** Year-round according to sowing date and type (calendar January–December). From sowing to cutting usually takes three to six months, varying with variety, size and weather. Slice through the stalk beneath the curd, leaving a few wrapper leaves attached.

**Storing:** Best eaten promptly. They will hold in a fridge for several days and keep better if a few leaves are left attached. Because they do not store well for long, avoid raising too many at once.

**Common problems:** Club root, cabbage caterpillars, cabbage root fly, cabbage whitefly, flea beetles, mealy cabbage aphid, white blister, pigeons, slugs and snails.

**In the kitchen:** White cauliflower makes a firm, ivory head of tightly packed, branching florets, typically 6–12 inches (15–30 cm) across, wrapped in long, dark-green leaves. The flavour is mild and neutral, so it takes equally to being eaten raw, steamed, roasted, fried or puréed, whole or broken into florets. Its origins are debated — Syria or Cyprus are the usual candidates — and it is a descendant of wild coastal Mediterranean cabbage.

### Kale

**Sow:** Raise plants under cover or in the open through spring into the start of summer (March–June). Indoors, ½in (1cm) deep in peat-free modules, two seeds thinned to one, grown in good light. Outdoors, often in a nursery row, ½in (1cm) deep and 4–6in (10–15cm) apart, for a move after 6–8 weeks. Also useful as a close-spaced salad crop, and as windowsill microgreens at any time of year, ready in two or three weeks. Rotate with other brassicas.

**Plant out:** Late spring or early summer (May–July) at 4–6in (10–15cm) tall. Harden off indoor plants, set them deeper so the lowest leaves sit at soil level, firm well – mature plants are top-heavy and need a firm anchorage – and water.

**Spacing:** For mature plants, 12–24in (30–60cm) apart according to variety; for baby-leaf salads, about 3in (7.5cm). One compact plant such as 'Dwarf Green Curled' will fill a 12in (30cm) pot; baby-leaf plants can be set 3in (7.5cm) apart in a large container. Stand pots in sun and keep them well watered in summer.

**Care:** Prefers rich, firm, well-drained ground but copes with most soils; or plant into a no-dig mulch. Water until established, then only in long dry spells; pots need regular watering. Mulch mature plants (less practical around baby-leaf rows). Feed is seldom needed except on poor soil or in pots, where an organic nitrogen-rich fertiliser helps. Weed to keep air moving between plants. In autumn, cane tall varieties on exposed sites. Keep insect-proof mesh over the crop against brassica pests and pigeons, and protect youngsters from slugs.

**Harvest window:** From September into spring (calendar October–April). As a cut-and-come-again salad, start once plants are 4–6in (10–15cm) tall.

**Storing:** Treat kale as a standing winter crop to pick as needed; no indoor store is given.

**Common problems:** Club root, cabbage caterpillars, cabbage root fly, cabbage whitefly, flea beetles, mealy cabbage aphid, white blister, pigeons, slugs and snails.

**In the kitchen:** Kale covers a wide family of hardy, open-leaved brassicas — broad or narrow, flat, frilled or curled, in greens and purples — with a prominent midrib and a crinkled surface. Young leaves are the ones for raw salads; older, chewier foliage is better cooked. It is one of the earliest offshoots of wild Brassica oleracea from the Near East and the eastern Mediterranean, once gathered as much for its seed as for the foliage.

**Varieties:**

- **'Starbor' AGM** (recommended)

### Kohl rabi

**Sow:** Early batches are best started under cover, because cold can make plants bolt before the stem swells. Indoors, two seeds ½in (1.5cm) deep per peat-free module, thinned to one; they usually come up in a couple of weeks and should be moved out after three to four weeks, while no more than 2in (5cm) tall. Outdoors in spring and summer (March–August), wait until temperatures stay above 50°F (10°C); spring sowings grow faster, late-summer ones crop in autumn and can run on into the first part of winter. Sow thinly in a ½in (1.5cm) drill, rows 1ft (30cm) apart, and thin to 6in (15cm) once seedlings are 1in (2.5cm) tall or show their first true leaf. Cover if a cold snap is forecast. A pot at least 1ft (30cm) wide and deep can be sown the same way, seed covered with ½in (1.5cm) of compost.

**Plant out:** April–May, while plants are still no more than 2in (5cm) tall – larger ones move less well – and only when outdoor temperatures stay above 50°F (10°C). Harden off gradually over a couple of weeks; a sudden chill can trigger bolting. Disturb the roots as little as possible.

**Spacing:** 6in (15cm) between plants, 1ft (30cm) between rows.

It does best in cool, damp weather and may run to flower in drought, in a chill, or in unusual heat. Weed, and cover with insect-proof mesh against cabbage butterflies and other brassica insects.

**Storing:** Pick young, and protect late roots in the ground; no separate indoor store is described.

**Common problems:** Bolting, club root, cabbage caterpillars, cabbage root fly, cabbage whitefly, flea beetles, mealy cabbage aphid, pigeons, slugs and snails.

**Varieties:**

- **'Korist' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Kossak'** (recommended)
- **'Olivia' AGM** (recommended)

### Pak choi

**Sow:** Indoors or out, in the ground or in large pots; seed usually appears in about a week. It prefers mild, damp weather in spring to early summer and late summer to autumn. Heat, cold and drought can trigger bolting, especially on plants grown to full size. Use cloches or a biodegradable fleece sheet if spring nights turn cold. For baby leaves, or in cooler districts, summer sowings are also possible. Indoors, one or two seeds per moist peat-free module, thinned to the strongest. Outdoors from late spring after frost risk (April–August), ¾in (2cm) deep, rows at least 1ft (30cm) apart: a warm, sheltered, sunny spot for early and late sowings, or cool light shade for summer crops. Protect seedlings from slugs and from cold.

**Plant out:** From late spring (calendar highlights May) once plants are at least 4in (10cm) tall. Harden off carefully so growth is not checked. **Storing:** Used fresh; no long store is described.

**Common problems:** Bolting, cabbage caterpillars, flea beetles, pigeons, slugs and snails.

**In the kitchen:** Pak choi (bok choy) is a bunched Chinese cabbage, typically 8–11 inches (20–27 cm) long in market types, with broad, spoon-shaped stems and loose, overlapping leaves. The flavour is green, slightly bitter and mineral; baby plants can be eaten raw, but most cooks give it a brief stir-fry or steam to soften the stems. It is native to China, probably around the Yangtze delta, after earlier brassicas arrived there from Central Asia and the eastern Mediterranean.

**Varieties:**

- **'Summer Breeze' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Choko F1'** (recommended)
- **'Glacier' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Ivory'** (recommended)
- **'Red Choi' AGM** (recommended)

### Radishes

**Sow:** Direct outdoors; seedlings usually appear in 7–10 days. Salad types: ½in (1cm) deep, 1–2in (2.5–5cm) apart, rows 6in (15cm) apart, March–September on the calendar; for an early picking, start in February in soil already warmed and covered with cloches. Short-rooted salad types also suit a pot at least 1ft (30cm) wide and deep. Winter types: July or August, about 1in (2cm) deep, seeds or thinnings 6–8in (15–20cm) apart, rows 6in (15cm) apart. Some guidance also notes ½in (1cm) deep and 4in (10cm) apart. Thin only if needed – salad types at least 1in (2.5cm) apart, larger winter and oriental types at least 6in (15cm) (one passage also says at least 5in / 15cm) – because crowded leafy tops can stop the root swelling. Fast salad radishes are useful gap-fillers and as row markers for slow seeds such as parsnips and onions.

**Plant out:** Usually sown where they are to grow; transplanting is not described.

**Spacing:** Salad types 1–2in (2.5–5cm) apart, rows 6in (15cm) apart. Winter types 6–8in (15–20cm) apart, rows 6in (15cm) apart.

**Care:** Open and sunny; light shade is acceptable for salad types in midsummer. Long winter and oriental types need deep soil (some roots run more than 1ft / 30cm). For no-dig, sow into a surface mulch. Keep the soil evenly moist so roots swell quickly and evenly and do not split; drought can stop swelling, and irregular watering can split them. Pots and young seedlings dry especially fast. Weed often – they dislike crowding – hoeing between rows and hand-weeding close in so a hoe does not wound the roots. They like warm but not baking conditions.

**Harvest window:** Salad types in as little as four to six weeks, at about 1in (2.5cm) across, while still mildly peppery; left larger, older or slow-grown they turn pithy, hot and poor to eat (calendar April–December, plus January–February for winter types). Winter radishes take at least eight weeks and may reach 1ft (30cm) or more; they can stay in the ground and be lifted through winter, with cloches, a biodegradable fleece sheet, or cardboard when frost is forecast. Oriental types make a long, mild root. Edible-podded types are grown for the young seed pods, which can be picked for many weeks, especially in warm weather.

**Storing:** Winter radishes can be lifted in November and kept in a fridge or similarly cool place, or left in the ground with frost protection.

**Common problems:** Bolting, brassica downy mildew, club root, cabbage root fly, flea beetles, slugs and snails.

**Varieties:**

- **'Longipinnatus'** (recommended)
- **'Scarlet Globe' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Black Spanish Round'** (recommended)
- **'French Breakfast 4 Francis' AGM** (recommended) — French Breakfast is an oblong heirloom radish, typically 2–4 inches (5–10 cm) long, with smooth fuchsia-red skin fading to a white tip and crisp, juicy white flesh. The flavour is mild, earthy and peppery, and cooking softens the heat. Eat it out of hand, on a platter, or — as in Victorian breakfasts — with toast, cress and potted shrimp. It was first recorded in Europe in 1879 and was a favourite on Paris market stalls.
- **'Amethyst' AGM** (recommended)
- **'China Rose'** (recommended)
- **Icicle radish** — Icicle is a slender, bright-white radish, about 4–6 inches (10–15 cm) long, tapering to a point, with dense, juicy white flesh that is mildly sweet and peppery. Shoulders left in the light may turn green. Slice or grate it raw into salads, pickle it, or cook it. It was recorded in European physic gardens in the 1600s and crossed the Atlantic in the late 19th century.
- **Sparkler radish** — Sparkler is a neat round radish, about 1–1½ inches (2.5–4 cm) across, scarlet on the upper two-thirds and creamy white at the base, with crisp, tender white flesh. Eat it raw on a platter or in a salad, layer it with butter in an open sandwich, or steam, braise or pickle it. The type was bred in the late 1800s and has been grown in Canada since the 1930s; it likes cool weather and dislikes extreme heat.
- **Black radish** — Black radishes come in two main shapes: a round type about 3–4 inches (7–10 cm) across, and a long type about 7–8 inches (17–20 cm). Both have thick, rough, almost black skin and white flesh with a sharp, horseradish-like heat. Slice or grate them thinly and balance the bite with something tart or sweet, or cook them to tame the spice. They were first grown in the eastern Mediterranean, stood cold winters well, and were known to the ancient Egyptians and Greeks.
- **Easter Egg radish** — Easter Egg radishes are a mixed handful of small, smooth roots, about ¾–1½ inches (2–4 cm) across, in pink, purple, red and white, with exceptionally crisp, moist white flesh and a mild peppery taste. Wash and eat them whole, or slice them onto buttered bread for colour on the plate. Radishes themselves come from wild plants in China and Central Asia; this mix is grown for its pretty skins rather than as a single historic variety.
- **Watermelon radish** — Watermelon radish is a small to medium daikon type, about 2–3 inches (5–8 cm) across, with ivory skin often blushed pink and green shoulders, and vivid pink-red flesh inside. The flavour is mild, slightly sweet and peppery, with the strongest heat in the skin and outer layers. Slice it raw to show the colour, or cook it. It is thought to have arisen near Beijing, after Mediterranean daikon types reached China around 500 BCE.
- **Daikon (mooli)** — Daikon, also sold as mooli, is a large, usually cylindrical radish from about 6–24 inches (15–60 cm) long, with firm white, purple or red skin and crisp, juicy flesh that feels heavy for its size. The taste is mildly sweet and peppery, and it is used raw, cooked or pickled — in Japan often as a refreshing accompaniment to richer dishes. The type is thought to have originated around the Mediterranean and the Black Sea, reaching China around 500 BCE and later becoming a staple in Japan, especially in the Edo period.

### Rocket

**Sow:** Indoors or out, in the ground or in pots, from spring to late summer, and later still with protection (calendar February–September). Under cover, sow thinly in small pots or modules of moist peat-free seed mix, or in a final pot at least 12in (30cm) wide; pot on once seedlings have a few leaves, and move them out at about 4in (10cm) tall. In the open, sow from April into the first part of September, then for one extra month with cloche or plastic-free fleece cover, in a shallow drill up to ½in (1cm) deep, rows 6in (15cm) apart, thinned to 6in (15cm) – crowding makes plants flower sooner. Pots, windowboxes, hanging baskets and growing bags all work; larger ones, at least 1ft (30cm) wide, dry out more slowly. Scatter seed thinly on the compost surface.

**Plant out:** When indoor or bought plants are about 4in (10cm) tall, from April to early September, after hardening off. In hot weather, set young transplants in a cooler, lightly shaded spot and water often, to limit wilting and bolting. For winter pickings, plant into a glasshouse or polytunnel border in autumn.

**Spacing:** 6in (15cm) between plants and between outdoor rows. The same spacing in a pot at least 1ft (30cm) wide.

**Care:** Fertile soil that holds some moisture; wild rocket also copes with poorer, drier ground. Weed and rake the bed, or sow into a no-dig mulch. Keep the soil moist in dry spells so growth stays even and plants do not run to seed early. Pots need regular watering in warm weather; move them into shade during hot spells to delay flowering. Avoid full sun at the height of summer for salad rocket. Weed to reduce competition for water. Guard against slugs. Salad rocket is usually treated as an annual and declines once it flowers; wild rocket is perennial and still yields usable leaves once it has flowered, through the remainder of the season and sometimes for several years.

**Harvest window:** From early summer into autumn, and into winter with protection (calendar April–December). Leaves can be ready about a month after sowing. Pick a few leaves from every plant as you work along the row, repeatedly, while they stay young and tender, until plants start to flower. Salad rocket is milder and more tender; wild rocket is hotter and can crop right through summer and autumn, and under cloches, in a cold frame or in a glasshouse it may continue into winter and start again in early spring.

**Storing:** Used fresh as a cut-and-come-again leaf; no long store is described.

**Common problems:** Bolting, flea beetles, slugs and snails.

**In the kitchen:** Rocket, or arugula, forms a loose rosette of deeply lobed, bright-green leaves, usually picked small at about 3–4 inches (7–10 cm), on fibrous, pale stems. The flavour is nutty and peppery, good raw or only lightly sautéed, blanched or stir-fried. It is a wild Mediterranean plant of southern Europe; the first written record dates from antiquity.

**Varieties:**

- **'Victoria'** (recommended)
- **'Sky Rocket'** (recommended)
- **'Runway'** (recommended)

### Swede

**Sow:** Best sown direct in the final bed, from the latter part of spring into early summer (April–June). Short batches a few weeks apart spread the harvest. Sow thinly in a ¾in (2cm) drill, rows 15in (38cm) apart; give early batches cloche or plastic-free fleece cover and keep slugs off the seedlings. Thin to 8–10in (20–25cm), or 3–4in (8–10cm) if you will pick them as baby roots. Full-sized plants are not suited to pots, but baby roots can be sown thinly in a container at least 16in (40cm) wide of peat-free compost, in a warm sunny place. Rotate with other brassicas.

**Plant out:** The calendar marks June, but sowing in the final position is recommended rather than transplanting.

**Spacing:** 8–10in (20–25cm) for full-sized roots, 3–4in (8–10cm) for baby veg, with 15in (38cm) between rows.

**Care:** An open place in sun, on fertile ground that stays moist yet drains freely, or sow into a no-dig mulch. The crop can take up to six months to mature. Water young plants until well rooted, then keep the soil evenly moist in dry spells; baby roots in pots need regular watering. Mulch thickly, especially on light, sandy ground. Weed while plants are young. Disease-resistant varieties are available, and insect-proof mesh helps against pests.

**Harvest window:** September–December, at whatever size you prefer: baby roots after only a few weeks, young roots about 2in (5cm) across, or, more usually, 4–6in (10–15cm). They can grow a little larger if you choose a variety that does not turn woody. Although hardy, insulate them with straw, cloches or a plastic-free fleece sheet ahead of winter.

**Storing:** In cold districts or on winter-wet soil, lift in late autumn and pack in damp sand or a peat-free potting mix, somewhere frost cannot reach, such as a shed or garage.

**Common problems:** Club root, cabbage caterpillars, cabbage root fly, flea beetles, powdery mildew, white blister, pigeons, slugs and snails.

**In the kitchen:** Swede (also called rutabaga, Swedish turnip, or neep in Scotland) is a swollen yellow-fleshed root, best at about 3–5 inches (7–12 cm) across and around 2 lb (1 kg); bigger ones can turn woody. The skin may be purple, bronze or green and must be peeled. Raw it is crisp, peppery and slightly sweet; cooked it becomes soft, mellow and sweeter than a true turnip, and is mashed, roasted, stewed or used in pasties, pies and the traditional Burns Night supper with haggis. It is thought to be a cabbage–turnip cross, first described in the wild in Sweden by Gaspard Bauhin in 1620, and reached England and Scotland in the 17th or 18th century.

**Varieties:**

- **'Gowrie' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Brora' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Magres' AGM** (recommended)

### Turnips

**Sow:** Main season March to mid-August; February sowings are possible under cloches or in a glasshouse (calendar February–August). Best sown direct, in the ground or in a large pot, but late-winter module sowings can be moved out while the roots are still small, for an early pick. Indoors, ¾in (2cm) deep: two seeds per module thinned to one, or up to four left as a mini-veg clump. Outdoors, thinly in a ½in (1cm) drill, rows 9–12in (23–30cm) apart. Thin to 4in (10cm) for baby roots or leafy tops; to 6in (15cm) for early varieties grown as larger roots, and 9in (23cm) for maincrops. Use leafy thinnings in salads. In a pot at least 16in (40cm) wide, cover seed with ¾in (2cm) of peat-free compost and thin to 4in (10cm) for mini-veg. Useful as a gap-filler after other crops, or between slower ones such as parsnips.

**Plant out:** Module-raised plants from March onwards (March–April), after hardening off, with as little root disturbance as possible, 6–9in (15–23cm) apart. Water in, and protect from slugs; use netting or biodegradable fleece if birds are a problem.

**Spacing:** 4in (10cm) for baby roots or greens; 6in (15cm) for early varieties as larger roots; 9in (23cm) for maincrops; 9–12in (23–30cm) between rows. Transplants 6–9in (15–23cm) apart.

**Care:** Sunny site and rich, well-drained soil; work in homemade compost or fully rotted manure, preferably during the previous autumn, or sow into a no-dig mulch. Water in dry weather or roots stay small and woody and may split. Pots need regular watering, especially in summer. Keep weed-free, particularly while plants are young.

**Harvest window:** May–December. Early types can be ready in about six weeks, maincrops in about ten, depending on conditions. Do not let roots grow larger than a tennis ball or they can turn woody and bitter. Young leaves are peppery raw; leaves up to 6in (15cm) can be cooked like spinach, but do not strip too many from plants grown for roots. You can also grow the crop purely for repeated greens.

**Storing:** Roots are not fully hardy, so lift late sowings before severe frost, or in mild districts leave them under cloches or plastic-free fleece and take them as needed through winter. Autumn-lifted roots can hold for some weeks in trays of damp coir or sand, somewhere cool and safe from frost.

**Common problems:** Club root, cabbage caterpillars, cabbage root fly, flea beetles, powdery mildew, white blister, pigeons, slugs and snails.

**In the kitchen:** Common turnips are small to medium roots, from about 2–8 inches (5–20 cm) across, round or slightly flattened, often with a thin taproot 3–4 inches (8–10 cm) long, and leafy green tops. Young roots can be eaten raw like a radish, sliced into salads and slaws; larger ones are cooked. They descend from a wild European and Asian species, with early records in Assyria and long cultivation across temperate regions.

**Varieties:**

- **'Atlantic'** (recommended)
- **'Temari Red' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Tokyo Cross' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Sweetbell'** (recommended)
- **'Oasis' AGM** (recommended)
- **Purple Top turnip** — Purple Top is the familiar garden turnip whose shoulders, sitting above the soil, colour bright purple while the buried part stays white. Smooth roots are about 3–4 inches (7–10 cm) across and are at their best young, mildly sweet with a hint of pepper; left to grow they turn bland and tough. They are often mixed up with swede, which is larger, with yellowish-purple skin and yellow flesh. The purple flush comes from anthocyanins.

## Roots and potatoes

### Beetroot

**Sow:** Mainly direct outdoors from late March or April to July once the soil is at least 45°F (7°C). Bolt-resistant varieties can go in from late February under fleece or cloches. Soak the corky seed clusters in warm water for an hour to help germination. Sow 1in (2.5cm) deep in short rows every few weeks for a long harvest. Indoors, especially in colder districts, sow one cluster (or up to four for baby clumps, left unthinned) 1in (2.5cm) deep in peat-free seed compost modules, thinning to the strongest seedling if several appear.

**Plant out:** Module-raised or bought plants can go out once they are at least 2in (5cm) tall and well rooted, after hardening off. Move the module intact and avoid disturbing the roots.

**Spacing:** Seed clusters 4in (10cm) apart, rows 1ft (30cm) apart; or groups of two or three seeds 6in (15cm) apart for clumps of baby roots. Single plugs 4in (10cm) apart; unsplit multi-sown modules 6in (15cm) apart. Containers should be at least 16in (40cm) wide and deep.

**Care:** Sunny, fertile, well-drained ground. Weed, then add garden compost or well-rotted manure, or sow or plant into a no-dig mulch. If the soil is slow to warm in spring, use cloches for a couple of weeks before early sowings. Water seedlings in dry weather until they are established; after that, a soak every 10–14 days in drought or on very free-draining soil is usually enough. Uneven watering can split roots, drought can make them woody, and too much water favours leaf at the expense of root. Pots need watering throughout. If growth is weak, use an organic high-nitrogen liquid feed. Hand-weed close to plants so a hoe does not nick the root shoulder.

**Harvest window:** June–October, according to sowing date and variety. Roots can be ready in as little as 40 days, more often 50–60. Flavour and texture are best at 1⅓–3in (4–7cm) across. Lift every other plant at golf-ball size and leave the rest until no bigger than a cricket ball; larger roots may go woody. Young leaves can be eaten raw or cooked, provided you leave four or five leaves on any plant whose root is still swelling.

**Storing:** June sowings lifted in autumn keep well. Choose larger, undamaged roots, twist or cut the leaves 1in (2.5cm) from the crown, and pack in dry sand, a peat-free potting mix or coir, in a shed or garage, for at least a couple of months. Where the ground will not freeze, late crops can stay in the soil until needed.

**Common problems:** Bolting (use bolt-resistant varieties for early or cold sowings; heat and drought can also trigger it), beet leaf miner, downy mildew. Woody roots if left too large or kept too dry.

**Varieties:**

- **'Wodan' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Babietola di Chioggia'** (recommended) — Chioggia, also called candy-stripe or bull’s-eye beet, is a small, slightly flattened Italian heirloom about 2 inches (5 cm) across, with burgundy skin and concentric rings of white and fuchsia inside. Raw it is crunchy, sweet and distinctly earthy — it is unusually high in geosmin, the compound behind that soil-like scent — so it is best sliced into salads or laid on soup, with a little lemon or vinegar to hold the stripes; cooking fades them to pale pink or white. It was found in the lagoon town of Chioggia, near Venice, in 1840 and reached the United States in the 1860s. The leaves, rather like young chard, are edible too.
- **'Forono' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Boltardy' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Cheltenham Green Top' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Pablo' AGM** (recommended)
- **Red beetroot** — Red beetroot is usually a globe about 1–3 inches (3–7 cm) across, with dark red-purple skin and dense, juicy crimson flesh that cooks tender, mild and earthy. It bleeds freely, staining boards and fingers, and is good raw in salads or roasted, boiled, steamed or fried; the leaves can be eaten like chard. Wild sea beet grew on Mediterranean and Atlantic coasts, and people first grew it for the greens; the root only became a food in its own right in the 1500s, once its sugar content was noticed. Detroit Red is among the best-known named types.
- **Bull's Blood** — Bull’s Blood is a 19th-century heirloom grown as much for its maroon-and-green leaves as for the dark red, slightly flattened roots, which are about 3–4 inches (7–10 cm) across on the bunched ‘baby’ plants. The even shape and smooth, easy-to-peel skin make it handy for even slices, whether shredded raw, roasted or pickled. It is thought to date from the late 19th century in Denmark, and an improved strain appeared in a Copenhagen seed list from J.E. Ohlsen Enke in the mid-20th century.

### Carrots

**Sow:** Sow direct outdoors only – moving plants disturbs the tap root and can cause forking. The main season is April to early July; earlier cultivars may go in during February or March if you cover the row with cloches or biodegradable fleece (calendar February–July). Do not sow on ground that has recently grown carrots, which may still hold carrot fly eggs. Make a shallow ½in (1cm) drill, water the base, and sow as thinly as you can. Repeat short sowings every three to four weeks. Germination can be slow. Thin to 2–3in (5–7.5cm) if needed (at least 2in / 5cm, as crowding distorts roots). Round, short types do well in pots of peat-free compost, again covered with about ½in (1cm) of compost.

**Plant out:** Not recommended; sow where the crop is to grow.

**Spacing:** Rows 6–12in (15–30cm) apart; thin plants to 2–3in (5–7.5cm).

**Care:** Weed thoroughly, fork out hard lumps and stones, and rake to a fine tilth, or, for no-dig, mulch and sow a short-rooted variety into the mulch. Plants are fairly drought-tolerant and seldom need water, but in a long dry spell a good soak every two weeks keeps growth even; big swings in moisture can split roots. Pots need regular watering. Cover the row completely with insect-proof mesh against carrot fly. Sowings after mid-May miss the first generation of the fly; lifting before late August misses the second. Rotate the crop. Hoe between rows but hand-weed close in, and avoid bruising the foliage (the scent attracts the fly); replace the mesh as soon as you finish.

**Harvest window:** May–November, typically 12–16 weeks after sowing. Lift as soon as roots are large enough; flavour often declines if you wait for very large roots. Spring sowings are at their best young, and baby carrots only a month or two old are especially sweet. Maincrop roots can be taken through summer into early autumn, or left in the ground into winter. Ease them out with a fork, especially on heavy soil, to avoid snapping.

**Storing:** In late autumn you can leave roots in the ground and lift as needed through winter, after removing the leaves and covering with straw or cardboard against frost. If the plot lies wet in winter, lift the remainder and store them.

**Common problems:** Splitting, carrot fly, mice and voles, slugs and snails, wireworms.

**Varieties:**

- **'Sweet Candle' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Adelaide' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Sugarsnax 54' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Flyaway' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Deep Purple' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Marion' AGM** (recommended)
- **Nantes carrot** — Nantes is a cylindrical orange carrot, typically 6–8 inches (15–20 cm) long and ¾–2 inches (2–5 cm) across, with blunt ends, smooth skin and fine, juicy flesh that has little or no woody core. Eat it raw without peeling, or roast, steam, bake or boil it; the leafy tops are edible too. Henri Vilmorin in France bred the type in the late 1850s, and it is still most at home in European markets.
- **Chantenay carrot** — Chantenay is a short, stout, conical orange carrot, about 4–5 inches (10–12 cm) long and 2–2½ inches (5–6 cm) across at the shoulder, with a crisp, sweet, earthy bite that softens but keeps its flavour in the pot. It is an all-purpose garden carrot for eating raw, roasting, steaming, canning or pickling, and its stocky shape suits heavier soils. The type was raised in the Chantenay district of France in the 18th century and listed by Vilmorin-Andrieux in the late 1800s.
- **Parisian carrot** — Parisian carrots are little round, radish-like orange roots, about ¾–2 inches (2–5 cm) across, with dense, crisp flesh and a mild, sweet, slightly grassy taste. They are a natural for roasting, glazing or serving whole as a nibble, and need no lengthy peeling. The type was developed in France around 1850, one of several round carrots bred there for flavour and size.
- **Purple carrot** — Purple carrots are tapered roots with dark purple or red-purple skin and a crisp orange or yellow core, tasting sweet and earthy with hints of celery and parsley, and sometimes a little pepper. Serve them raw if you want to keep the colour; boiling or baking can turn the purple to a dull grey. These are among the oldest cultivated carrots, domesticated more than 5,000 years ago in what is now Afghanistan, before orange types became the market standard.
- **Rainbow carrots** — Rainbow carrots are simply a mixed bunch of red, yellow, purple and orange roots, crunchy when raw and sweet, becoming nuttier and soft-buttery once cooked. Use them as you would ordinary carrots, and keep some raw if you want the colours to stay bright. The source page describes a California-grown mix and does not give a separate history for the blend.
- **Yellow carrot** — Young yellow carrots are slender cones about 4–5 inches (10–12 cm) long, with smooth pale-gold skin and crisp yellow flesh that is mildly sweet and less earthy than orange types. Eat them raw in salads and slaws, or glaze, boil and roast them; they also pickle well. Yellow carrots, like the purple ones, originated more than 5,000 years ago in Central Asia and later travelled into Africa and Europe.

### Celeriac

**Sow:** Indoors only in the UK – the crop needs at least six months to mature, and an outdoor sowing would be too late. Sow thinly in March (calendar March–April) in small pots or modules of peat-free seed compost, barely covered, at 59°F (15°C) in a heated propagator or under a clear bag. Germination can be slow and uneven, up to three weeks. Grow on in bright light above 50°F (10°C); cooler conditions can cause later bolting. Keep seedlings watered, thin to one per module, and pot on from trays into individual pots of peat-free multi-purpose compost.

**Plant out:** Late May or early June, after the last frost. Young plants are cold-sensitive, so delay if it is still chilly, and harden off. Do not bury the crown – the stem base should sit at soil level. Water in, then give a cloche or biodegradable fleece cover so the plants stay warm and are less likely to bolt.

**Spacing:** 1ft (30cm) between plants, 16–18in (40–45cm) between rows, with room for air movement.

**Care:** Sun or light shade, in ground that stays evenly moist without lying waterlogged. Add plenty of garden compost, or plant into a no-dig mulch. As plants mature, gradually remove outer leaves once they fall horizontal, to expose the crown; rub off side-shoots and any damaged or spotted leaves (possible leaf miner or leaf spot). If the soil dries, give a thorough soak rather than frequent dribbles. Mulch thickly, keeping material off the crown. Keep weed-free.

**Harvest window:** October–March. Plants are hardy and can usually stay in the ground; flavour strengthens with time, and large roots need not turn woody. Ease them out with a fork. In colder spots, a thick straw mulch stops the soil freezing solid and making lifting difficult. Trim off the small roots and knobbly skin before cooking. Spare roots can be blanched and frozen.

**Storing:** If you need the bed in spring, lift remaining plants and either replant them elsewhere or twist off the leaves and pack the crowns in peat-free potting compost or coir in a cool shed or garage. On heavy or winter-wet ground, lift and store in late autumn.

**Common problems:** Bolting, celery leaf mining fly.

**In the kitchen:** Celeriac, or celery root, is a knobbly swollen stem base, typically 4–5½ inches (10–14 cm) across, with a rough brown exterior and dense ivory flesh that browns once cut (a soak in acidulated water stops this). Raw it is crisp, nutty and herbal, less starchy than a potato; cooked it becomes smooth and tender, excellent mashed, roasted, in soups or in the classic French céleri rémoulade. It is a different variety from stalk celery, well known in Europe by the 17th century, and will keep 6–8 months in proper store.

**Varieties:**

### Chicory

**Sow:** Do not sow too early outdoors – cold spells can cause bolting. Forcing types: outdoors in May and June. Non-forcing types: under cover in April and May, outdoors in June and July. For cut-and-come-again baby leaves, sow non-forcing cultivars in the open through spring and summer; under glass, a late-winter sowing gives an early pick and a late-summer or early-autumn sowing supplies winter leaves. Indoor sowings in moist peat-free seed mix germinate in a few days on a warm bright sill or in a heated propagator; pot on into modules once seedlings have several leaves. Outdoors, sow thinly ½in (1cm) deep. Calendar: sow February–October.

**Plant out:** From late May or early June, delaying if it is still cold (cold encourages bolting). Harden off, water before and after planting, and protect from slugs. Late-summer sowings of non-forcing types can be moved into a greenhouse border or large pot in autumn for winter cut-and-come-again leaves.

**Spacing:** Forcing types 6in (15cm) apart; non-forcing heading types 1ft (30cm) apart (radicchio needs at least this for the rounded head); baby-leaf plants 2in (5cm) apart. A container should be at least 1ft (30cm) wide.

**Care:** Open and sunny, with some summer shade acceptable between taller crops. Weed and dig in garden compost or well-rotted manure a few weeks ahead, or use a no-dig mulch. Water well in dry summer weather to limit bolting and bitterness. Mulch once plants are growing, and give container plants a general liquid feed every two weeks through summer. Open-ground plants usually need no feed. Forcing types such as 'Witloof': in November lift roots at least 1in (2.5cm) across at the crown, cut the leaves back to 1in (2.5cm), pot the dormant roots and blanch in the dark, as for forced rhubarb, for winter and early-spring chicons.

**Harvest window:** Forced chicons after about four weeks of blanching, at 6in (15cm) tall, through winter and early spring. Non-forcing radicchio and sugarloaf heads from late summer to mid-autumn (calendar also lists July and winter months for leaf crops); cut the head and leave the stump, which may throw a smaller second head. Take heading types before the first frost, or give them cloche or plastic-free fleece cover. Discard tough outer leaves; inner leaves taste sweeter if kept out of light after cutting or they green up and turn more bitter. Radicchio often colours as days shorten. Baby leaves can be taken from early summer.

**Storing:** Used fresh, or forced in winter, rather than stored as heads.

**Common problems:** Bolting, aphids, grey mould, powdery mildew, slugs and snails.

**Varieties:**

- **'Leonardo'** (recommended)
- **'Palla Rossa' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Pan di Zucchero' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Witloof Zoom'** (recommended)

Shred it raw into salads or cook it to soften the bitterness. It is native to Italy, where wild forms were once regarded as poor man’s food.

### Parsnips

**Sow:** Direct outdoors only – the tap root does not transplant well. Wait until the soil is around 54°F (12°C); sowings in March, April and even early May succeed more reliably than February attempts except in very mild districts. Fresh seed is important. Warm and dry the bed with cloches for a couple of weeks first if you can. Sow thinly in a ½in (1cm) drill, or sow three seeds every 6in (15cm), then cover. Germination is slow, often 14–30 days. Fleece or cloches over seedlings for a few weeks help them away. Thin to the strongest seedling at each station.

**Plant out:** Not recommended; sow where the crop is to grow.

**Spacing:** Rows 1ft (30cm) apart; aim for a plant every 6in (15cm) after thinning.

**Care:** Open, sunny site with deep, light, free-draining soil. Do not sow on ground that recently grew parsnips or carrots (carrot fly), and keep the two crops apart. Remove stones and loosen heavy or compacted soil so roots run straight. Do not dig in organic matter just before sowing, which can cause forking; ground manured or composted the previous year is ideal. For no-dig, sow into a surface mulch. Water young plants in dry spells; once established they are fairly drought-tolerant, but even moisture helps prevent splitting. Mulch when the soil is warm and damp. Hand-weed close to the crowns (a hoe can wound them and invite canker) and avoid bruising the leaves, which can attract carrot fly. Cover with insect-proof mesh from the start. Once the canopy closes it smothers many weeds.

**Harvest window:** September–March, once the leaves begin to die back in late summer or autumn. Waiting until after the first hard frost sweetens the roots. Mark the row before the foliage vanishes. Ease the long roots out with a fork. They are fully hardy and can stay in the ground through winter; lift a few extras in November, or insulate the bed with straw, so you can still harvest if the soil freezes.

**Storing:** Leaving roots in the ground is the usual method. The November lift or a straw cover is for convenience in hard frost rather than a separate indoor store.

**Common problems:** Splitting, carrot fly. Wound canker if the root shoulder is damaged.

**In the kitchen:** Parsnips are long, tapered roots that can reach about 1 foot (30 cm) long and 3 inches (8 cm) across at the crown, with yellow-beige to white skin, creamy-white fine-grained flesh, and a notably sweet, nutty flavour. High natural sugar makes them equally at home in savoury mashes, roasts and soups or in sweet dishes — the source even suggests a milk-and-sugar purée frozen as ice cream. They are an eastern Mediterranean plant, a winter staple in Europe long before the potato, and were taken to the Americas by colonists in 1609.

### Potatoes

**Sow:** Grown from seed tubers, not true seed. First and second earlies can be chitted in egg boxes or trays in a cool, bright place, beginning toward the end of January in milder districts and as late as the first part of March where it is colder; sprouting takes four to six weeks and brings earlies on sooner, but is not essential. There is little benefit to chitting maincrops.

**Plant out:** First earlies around late March, second earlies in early to mid-April, maincrops from mid-April to early May, later in cold districts and earlier in mild ones (calendar March–May). For an extra-early crop, plant chitted earlies at the beginning of March in large containers in a frost-free glasshouse, for a lift around mid-May. For a winter or Christmas crop, set cold-stored tubers in a large pot under cover during July or the first part of August, which also helps against blight. Young shoots are easily frosted in April and May, so choose an open, sunny site not prone to late frosts.

**Spacing:** Plant in a 6in (15cm) trench (or individual holes) with sprouts facing up. Earlies 1ft (30cm) apart, rows 2ft (60cm) apart; maincrops 15in (37cm) apart, rows 30in (75cm) apart. In pots at least 12in (30cm) wide and deep, half-filled with 6in (15cm) of peat-free compost, plant one tuber per 12in (30cm) of pot diameter just below the surface, then earth up with more compost as shoots grow. No-dig growers can set tubers shallowly and cover with a deep organic mulch, lifting later from the mulch.

**Care:** Rich, fertile ground: dig in plenty of garden compost or well-rotted manure the previous autumn or winter if you can, or after planting spread a 4in (10cm) layer – suitable for no-dig. A general organic fertiliser can lift yields on poor soil. Earth up when shoots are about 9in (23cm) tall, leaving the top 4in (10cm) showing, and repeat every few weeks to a final ridge of 8–12in (20–30cm); this protects shoots from late frost and stops tubers greening. If you cannot earth up you should still get a crop. Water in dry weather; containers need regular watering even in rain, because the canopy can shed water. Open-ground plants usually need no feed, but maincrops on poor soil benefit from a nitrogen-rich organic fertiliser. Rotate the crop each year.

**Harvest window:** June–October. Check tuber size by gently scraping soil away before lifting a whole plant. First earlies in June–July, second earlies in July–August, typically when flowers are open or buds drop and tubers are about hen's-egg size. Maincrops from late August to October; start lifting in late summer for immediate use, or leave healthy crops until mid-autumn. Insert the fork at least 1ft (30cm) from the stem. Discard green tubers, which can be harmful.

**Storing:** Maincrops often keep for several months. Store only dry, undamaged tubers with the soil brushed off, in darkness (to limit sprouting) in paper or hessian bags or on slatted trays, somewhere dry, cool and safe from frost – a garage is typical. Check every few weeks for rot, and use them before early spring, when they start to sprout and shrivel.

**Common problems:** Plant viruses, potato blight, black scurf and stem canker, blackleg, scab, tuber rots, potato cyst nematodes, slugs and snails, wireworms.

### Salsify

**Sow:** Direct outdoors in the final position once the soil is warming in spring (March–May). Use fresh seed, as viability falls quickly, as with parsnips. Do not transplant, which can fork the roots. Sow thinly in a ½in (1cm) drill, or sow two or three seeds every 4in (10cm) and thin to one. Germination is often slow and uneven, taking two or three weeks. The same method works in a pot at least 1ft (30cm) across and as deep, filled with damp peat-free multi-purpose mix.

**Plant out:** Not recommended; sow where the crop is to grow.

**Spacing:** Thin to 4in (10cm) apart, with 1ft (30cm) between rows.

**Care:** Sunny site with light, deep, free-draining soil – roots can run to 1ft (30cm). Clear stones, loosen heavy or compacted ground, and avoid recently manured or very stony soil, which can fork the roots. Ground improved with garden compost for the previous crop is ideal; for no-dig, sow into well-rotted surface mulch. Little maintenance is needed and no feed is required. Water seedlings if it is dry; once established, water only in long droughts to limit splitting. Pots need regular watering. Mulch with garden compost in late spring. Hoe between rows and hand-weed close to the crowns. Protect young plants from slugs and snails.

**Harvest window:** October–April. Roots are usually ready from mid-autumn but can stay in the ground through winter into early spring; frost sweetens them, as with parsnips. Ease the slender roots out with a fork – they snap and bleed easily. From late autumn, insulate plants left in the ground with straw or plastic-free fleece. Cook first, then peel, because raw roots exude a sticky latex and the skin is tough. If some roots are left, they resprout in spring: cut old leaves back to 1in (2.5cm) and blanch the new shoots under a 4–6in (10–15cm) mound of soil (or straw or an upturned bucket on heavy ground) for an extra crop.

**Storing:** In very cold districts or on winter-wet soil, lift roots and pack them in sand or sawdust somewhere cool and frost-free, such as a garage or cellar. Otherwise leave them in the ground and lift as needed.

**Common problems:** Bolting, splitting, slugs and snails.

**In the kitchen:** Salsify is a long, slender, parsnip-shaped root; white salsify has hairy tan-brown skin, while black salsify (scorzonera) is smoother and almost black. The white flesh should be peeled and dropped into acidulated water so it does not discolour, then steamed, puréed, roasted or fried. White salsify is a plant of the east Mediterranean, first taken into gardens in Italy and France, and later fashionable in North America during the 1700s; black salsify has a broader European and Asian range and was first grown in Spain.

**Varieties:**

- **'Mammoth'** (recommended)

### Sweet potatoes

**Sow:** Usually grown from cuttings (slips) or rooted plugs bought in spring, not from seed. Pot them at once into small pots of peat-free compost, or stand slips in a glass of water until they root, then keep them warm, bright and humid in a propagator or under a clear bag, watered with tepid water, and pot on as they grow. You can also sprout saved, sound tubers in damp sand, with gentle heat – a propagator or airing cupboard in spring – and when shoots are 2–3in (5–7.5cm) long cut them off and root them in compost. Calendar planting of slips: March–April.

**Plant out:** Late spring or early summer (May–June) once plants are well rooted and growing strongly, ideally into a greenhouse, mini-greenhouse or cold frame. Outdoors, only try a very warm, sunny, sheltered spot, preferably under cloches or plastic-free fleece, after hardening off. Warm outdoor soil with cloches or fleece for a few weeks first.

**Spacing:** At least 1ft (30cm) apart, at the same depth as in the pot. Containers should be at least 1ft (30cm) wide, one plant in the centre, or use a grow bag.

**Care:** These tropical plants need warmth and steady moisture. In the open ground, fork in about two buckets of compost or well-rotted manure per square yard (square metre), or plant into a no-dig mulch. Keep soil or compost evenly moist; pots may need daily watering in hot weather or tubers will not swell. A pot sunk beside each plant makes a useful watering well once the vines sprawl. Mulch outdoor plants. Prepared ground usually needs no feed; in containers, give a potassium-rich organic liquid feed once a fortnight all summer. Weed while plants are young; established vines usually smother weeds. They can sprawl or be trained up a 6ft (1.8m) wigwam, wall wires, trellis, or greenhouse strings; tie in stems as needed and trim any that outgrow their space (young stems and leaves are edible).

**Harvest window:** September–October, after roughly four or five months of growth, when the foliage yellows and collapses in early autumn and before frost. Tip plants from pots or ease them with a fork, handling the delicate skins gently; use damaged tubers at once. Yield depends on warmth, moisture and the length of the growing season.

**Storing:** Dry the skins in warm sun for a few days to help them keep, but they generally last only a few weeks, so lift one or two plants at a time and check stored tubers often for rot.

**Common problems:** Glasshouse red spider mite, glasshouse whitefly.

**Varieties:**

- **'Beauregard'** (recommended)
- **'Bonita'** (recommended)
- **'Evangeline'** (recommended)
- **'Tahiti'** (recommended)

## Alliums

### Garlic

**Sow:** Not usually grown from seed. Plant cloves. Typical cultivars require a chilly spell lasting four to eight weeks at 32–50°F (0–10°C) before they form good bulbs, so the main planting window is mid-autumn to early winter (October–December). Some types can go in during late winter and early spring (February–March).

**Plant out:** On heavy or winter-wet soil, start cloves in module trays in autumn, overwinter them somewhere frost-sheltered and rain-protected (a cold glasshouse, frame, or a quiet outdoor corner), and move them into their final positions in spring.

**Spacing:** Cloves 6in (15cm) apart, tips 1in (2.5cm) below the surface, rows 1ft (30cm) apart. In light soil, slightly deeper planting can give larger bulbs; do not plant deeply on heavy ground. Pointed end up, flat basal plate down.

**Care:** Sunny, free-draining ground; wet soil, especially for autumn planting, raises disease risk. Avoid very acid soil (below pH 6); lime in autumn or winter if a test shows it is needed. Work in about two buckets of well-rotted (not fresh) manure or garden compost per square yard (square metre), or plant through a no-dig mulch; a biodegradable weed-suppressing membrane can cut later weeding. Cover newly planted cloves with plastic-free netting or fleece so birds cannot pull them out. Water in dry spells in spring and early summer to bulk up bulbs, then stop once foliage yellows and bulbs are well formed, to reduce rotting; try not to wet the leaves. Weed by hand, or mulch with cardboard between rows, so plants are not shaded. Snip off flower stems as they appear (young scapes are edible) so energy goes into the bulb and storage quality is not reduced.

**Harvest window:** May–August. Autumn-planted crops are ready from late spring to early summer, spring-planted ones from mid-summer to early autumn, when the leaves turn yellow. Do not leave lifting late or bulbs tend to open and store less well. Ease them up gently with a fork; bruising also shortens storage life. Green leaves, and any 'top set' cloves on the stem, can be used while plants are still growing.

**Storing:** Spread the bulbs out, unsstacked, in sunshine to dry – an airy glasshouse works well if you keep the temperature below 86°F (30°C) – or use a dry, airy shed, for two to four weeks. When the leaves are dry and rustling, trim them and keep the bulbs cool and dry at 41–50°F (5–10°C). Hardnecks often keep four to six months; softnecks may keep up to a year.

**Common problems:** Allium leaf miner, leek rust, onion downy mildew, onion white rot, leek moth, onion fly.

**Varieties:**

- **'Arno'** (recommended)
- **'Cristo' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Germidour' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Solent Wight' AGM** (recommended)
- **Garlic (softneck)** — Ordinary softneck garlic makes medium to large bulbs, typically 2–3 inches (5–8 cm) across, with several layers of cloves in papery wrappers. A whole bulb smells only mildly of allium; crushing or chopping releases allicin and the familiar pungent kick, which mellows and sweetens if you roast it. Use it raw in dressings or cooked in almost any savoury dish, and store it for 1–4 months in a cool, dry place. Softnecks, especially types such as California Early and Late, dominate the shops because they are easy to grow and keep well; garlic has a long cultural history, from Egyptian tombs to kitchens across Europe and Asia.
- **Elephant garlic** — Elephant garlic is not a true garlic but a kind of leek, forming a huge bulb about 4 inches (10 cm) across that can weigh up to 1 lb (about 450 g), usually with around five mild, yellowish cloves. The taste sits between onion, leek and a soft garlic note, so it can be sliced for salads, roasted whole as a spread, or fried into chips; crushing it sharpens the flavour. Immigrants from Central Europe were growing it in Oregon when the Nicholas Garden nursery spotted it and put it on the market in 1941, later renaming it from Giant to Elephant.
- **Spanish Roja** — Spanish Roja is a rocambole hardneck, usually 2–3 inches (5–7 cm) across, with a woody central stem, white to purple-striped wrappers and a ring of six to eight large cloves in easy-to-peel red-brown skins. Raw it is fiery and rich with a thread of sweetness; cooking deepens it into a savoury, lingering garlic flavour, excellent minced into butter, sauces and dressings or roasted with meat and vegetables. It is thought to have come from Spain and reached North American gardens in the 1800s; like other rocamboles it is prized for taste but keeps only a short time, so it is grown on a small scale.
- **Music** — Music is a large porcelain hardneck, from about ¾–3 inches (2–8 cm) across depending on the season, with four to seven fat, easy-to-peel cloves in burgundy or bronze skins. The flavour is robust, musky and a little floral and peppery, good raw in sauces or roasted until sweet and spreadable, and the plants also throw useful scapes in early summer. Toronto taxi driver Al Music is said to have brought the bulbs back from Italy in the 1980s and grown them on his farm in Norfolk County, Ontario; the variety likes a cold climate and is now widely planted in Canada and the Pacific Northwest.
- **German White** — German White (also called Northern White or German Extra-Hardy) is a big porcelain hardneck, about 3½ inches (9 cm) across, with four to six large cloves in ivory skins, the clove wrappers whitish-tan with purple stripes that deepen in cold weather. It has a strong, lasting heat that softens in the pan, and a single clove can flavour a whole dish; use it in pesto, soups, butters and roasts, and the plants also give edible scapes. It is thought to come from northern Germany, stands cold winters well, and will keep for as long as 10 months in a cool, dark place.
- **Garlic scapes** — Garlic scapes are the young flowering stems of hardneck garlic, long, slender and often coiled, with a small bud (the spathe) at the tip. Picked early they are tender and crisp, cooking rather like asparagus, with a fresh, green garlic scent; later they turn fibrous. Trim the ends, slip off the spathe, and slice them into pesto, butter, salads or a simple sauté. Growers have long snapped them off to send the plant’s energy into larger bulbs, and they remain a short spring treat rather than a year-round crop.

### Leeks

**Sow:** Indoors from late winter on a windowsill or in a greenhouse: sow thinly about ¾in (1.5cm) deep in small pots or modules of peat-free seed compost, kept just moist, in good light and above 50°F (10°C); pot on if roots appear through the drainage holes. Ready to move after about eight weeks, at 8in (20cm) tall. Outdoors, the main months are March and April, either in a nursery seedbed for later transplanting or in the final row; later-cropping varieties can still be sown into June. Calendar: sow February–June.

**Plant out:** Transplant when plants are about 8in (20cm) tall and pencil-thick, in May–July. Harden off indoor-raised plants. Make holes about 6in (15cm) deep and 2in (5cm) across with a dibber, stand one plant in each (roots trimmed to 1in / 2.5cm), and 'puddle in' with water rather than backfilling. Deep planting helps form a long white shank.

**Spacing:** Holes 6–8in (15–20cm) apart, or 4in (10cm) apart for baby leeks, with 1ft (30cm) between rows. The same spacing can be used in large pots of peat-free compost.

**Care:** Sunny, free-draining ground where alliums have not been grown for at least a few years. Dig in two buckets of compost or well-rotted manure per square yard (square metre), or use a no-dig mulch. Water young plants until they are established, then only in long dry spells; try not to wet the leaves (rust risk). Containers need regular watering. Mulch to hold moisture. Weed often – foliage casts little shade – hoeing between rows and hand-weeding close in. Earth up as plants grow to lengthen the white base. If a flower stalk appears, remove it promptly or the shank turns tough; tight buds (scapes) are good to eat.

**Harvest window:** August–March. Baby leeks can be ready in about two months; full-sized plants may take six months. Start lifting from late summer, taking alternate plants so the rest can bulk up. Late varieties can stay in the ground through winter; frost may sweeten the flavour. Lift with a fork as needed.

**Storing:** The plot is the store: lift what you need and leave the remainder in the ground, including through winter for late types. No separate indoor storage method is given.

**Common problems:** Bolting, allium leaf miner, leek rust, onion white rot, leek moth, onion fly, slugs and snails.

**In the kitchen:** The common leek forms a thick bundle of leaf sheaths — a white-to-green ‘pseudostem’ with a fan of tougher blue-green leaves — and can stand about 3 feet (1 m) tall. Raw it is crisp with a mild, sweet onion flavour; cooked it turns tender and succulent. Wash well between the layers, then use the paler parts in salads or soften the whole stem in soups such as vichyssoise and cock-a-leekie, or in tarts, quiches and potato dishes. It is an ancient Mediterranean and Middle Eastern vegetable, eaten in Egypt around 2000 BCE and later spread through Europe by the Romans.

**Varieties:**

- **'Porvite' (Autumn Giant 2 Group)** (recommended)
- **'Krypton' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Stamford' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Oarsman' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Blauwgroene Winter' – Bandit AGM** (recommended)
- **'Porbella' AGM** (recommended)
- **French leek** — French leeks (sometimes sold as French leek Primor) are slimmer than ordinary leeks, with more snowy-white shank and less fibrous green, and a savoury-sweet flavour without much allium heat. Young plants may be about 1 foot (30 cm) long and mature ones up to 2 feet (60 cm), ideally only ½ inch (13 mm) across and no more than ¾ inch (19 mm). They are mild enough to eat raw, but also stand long cooking in winter tarts, pot pies and soups, or a quick sauté with potatoes. The type was bred in France as a uniform, early, non-bulbing crop for spring and early summer, and is still better known there than elsewhere.

### Onions

**Sow:** From seed, under cover from mid-winter to early spring (January–April on the calendar), about ½in (1.3cm) deep in modules of peat-free seed mix, one seed per cell, or three or four together if you accept smaller bulbs; germinate with gentle heat or on a bright, warm indoor sill, then grow on in good light, slightly moist. Overwintering (Japanese) seed such as 'Toughball' can be started under cover in August or September. Sets are the usual alternative to seed.

**Plant out:** Harden off seedlings in spring, and do the same in early autumn if you are raising overwintering types, before they go into the bed. Sets usually go out in early to mid-spring (March–April) for a July–September harvest, or in autumn (September–November) for overwintering (Japanese) types aimed at June–July. If the soil is very wet and cold, start sets in modules under cover and move them out when conditions improve; skip autumn planting on heavy, winter-waterlogged ground or the sets may rot.

**Spacing:** Seed-raised plants about 4in (10cm) apart, or 6in (15cm) if you want large bulbs, in rows 10–12in (25–30cm) apart; leave multi-sown modules unsplit. Sets ¾in (2cm) deep so the tip is just visible, 4–6in (10–15cm) apart (wider for larger bulbs), rows 8–12in (20–30cm) apart. Cover with fleece or netting until rooted, as birds often pull sets out.

**Care:** Sunny, free-draining ground. Dig in a bucket of garden compost or well-rotted manure (not fresh) per square yard (square metre), or use a no-dig mulch; onions have a limited root system. Water in long dry spells while roots are forming, then stop once bulbs have swollen in mid-summer – later watering of spring-planted crops can worsen storage. Avoid wetting the leaves. On poor soil, an organic nitrogen-rich feed in late winter can help autumn-planted onions and may reduce premature flowering. Keep weed-free, preferably by hand close to the bulbs. Remove flower stems at once or bulbs will not store well.

**Harvest window:** June–September (seed-raised and spring-planted crops can run into October). Autumn-planted sets are ready in June and July; spring-planted sets and seed-raised onions from July to October. Yellowing, toppling foliage is the cue; lift before the leaves die completely. Do not bend the tops or break the roots – that is no longer advised. Ease bulbs up carefully and use any damaged ones at once.

**Storing:** Dry for about two weeks in full sun on wire racks, or in a glasshouse or airy shed if it is damp. When the skins are papery, keep them in net bags, in a single tray layer, or in plaits, somewhere light, cool, dry and airy – darkness encourages sprouting. Autumn-planted bulbs last into the first part of winter; those planted in spring, or raised from seed, can hold until well into the next spring.

**Common problems:** Bolting, allium leaf miner, leek rust, onion downy mildew, onion neck rot, onion white rot, leek moth, onion fly, slugs and snails.

**Varieties:**

- **'Centurion' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Red Baron' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Hercules' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Marshalls Red Fen'** (recommended)
- **'Sturon' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Rumba' AGM** (recommended)
- **Red onion** — Red onions are typically about 4–6 inches (10–15 cm) across, wrapped in papery purple skin, with crisp white-and-purple rings that taste pungent, sweet and a little spicy when raw, then milder and sweeter once cooked. They are the usual choice for slicing over salads, burgers and sandwiches, and they also grill, roast, caramelise or pickle well. The type is linked with several historic growing areas, including Tropea in Italy, Turda in Romania and Wethersfield in Connecticut, and is now planted widely in Europe and the Americas; whole bulbs keep 1–2 months in a cool, dry place.
- **White onion** — White onions are medium to large globes under bright, flaky white skin, with juicy, almost translucent rings that are crisp, mildly sweet and sharp, without a harsh aftertaste. Use them raw in salads, salsas and guacamole, or roast, grill, fry and add them to soups, stews and casseroles; they are a mainstay of Mexican cooking, from street tacos to huevos rancheros. The crop is an old Asian cultivated onion, now grown worldwide, and sound bulbs will store for 1–2 months.
- **Yellow onion** — Yellow onions, usually 2–4 inches (5–10 cm) across, have coppery, papery skins and crisp, juicy white flesh that is fierce when raw and turns nutty and sweet once cooked. They are the everyday cooking onion — the base of French onion soup, stews, stocks, gratins and slow caramelising — and dominate northern European production as well as most of the American crop. Onions have been grown for some 5,000 years and are thought to have spread from Asia; whole bulbs keep 1–2 months in a cool, airy place.
- **Pickling onion** — Pickling onions are small, typically ¾–2 inches (2–5 cm) across, with thin papery skins in white, brown or red and crisp, juicy flesh that is sweeter and less fierce than a full-sized bulb. They are, of course, excellent pickled whole as a British accompaniment to pies, cheese and cold meats, but they can also be roasted, dropped whole into stews, or chopped raw into salads and salsas. Types grouped under the name include Barletta, Paris Silverskin and various pearl or button onions; pickling vegetables became especially popular from the 18th century, though small onions have been used for far longer.
- **Roscoff onion** — Roscoff is a protected pink onion from Brittany, usually 1½–3 inches (4–8 cm) across, often sold in braids with at least 2 inches (5 cm) of dried stem left on. Copper-pink skins hide white, pink-tinged flesh that is crunchy, juicy and mildly sweet with a fruity scent; it is good raw in salads and dressings, and it cooks quickly into soup, quiche, tarts and chutney. A Capuchin monk, Brother Cyril, is said to have sown Portuguese seed in the convent garden at Roscoff in 1647; sailors later prized the bulbs on long voyages as a defence against scurvy.
- **Rossa di Tropea** — Rossa di Tropea is a protected red onion from the Calabrian coast, often the long, torpedo-shaped Allungata type, typically 3–4 inches (7–10 cm) long and about 2–2½ inches (5–6 cm) across. The papery purple-brown skins cover crisp, juicy red-purple rings that are unusually mild and sweet, with little of the usual onion bite, so they are often eaten raw with bread, oil, cheese and cured meats, or simmered into jams and chutneys. The crop has been grown in southern Italy since antiquity and is thought to have arrived with Phoenician and Greek traders.
- **Walking onion** — Walking onions (also called Egyptian, tree or topset onions) send up hollow green stalks 2–3 feet (0.6–1 m) tall, topped with clusters of small bulbils about ⅜–2 inches (1–5 cm) across; the weight of the cluster bends the stem to the ground, which is how the plant ‘walks’. Leaves, bulbils and the underground bulbs are all edible — use the greens like chives or spring onions, and cook or pickle the bulbils as you would a mild onion. Most accounts treat it as a cultivated hybrid of Welsh onion and ordinary bulbing onion, with roots in Asia; it had reached England before the 16th century and remains a garden curiosity rather than a commercial crop.

### Salad onions

**Sow:** Usually direct outdoors from March, in short batches every couple of weeks, for summer and autumn picking; hardy varieties sown in late summer and early autumn (through September on the calendar) stand the winter as small plants and can be pulled from March. Sow thinly in a ½in (1cm) deep drill, then thin to about 1in (2.5cm) – closer spacing can encourage downy mildew; use the thinnings in salads. In March, if the soil is still cold and wet, start seed under cover in modules for later transplanting, or sow in a glasshouse border or a generous pot as the final home.

**Plant out:** Module-raised or bought plants can go out once they are well rooted and growing strongly, after hardening off in early to mid-spring. Water before and after planting, a few centimetres apart.

**Spacing:** Rows about 8in (20cm) apart; thin seedlings to about 1in (2.5cm).

**Care:** Sunny, free-draining ground. Dig in a bucket of garden compost or well-rotted manure (not fresh) per square yard (square metre), rake out lumps and stones, or use a no-dig mulch. Water young plants until they are established, then only in long dry spells; pots need more frequent watering. Keep weed-free, hoeing between rows and hand-weeding in the row. Autumn-sown hardy types often benefit from cloches or biodegradable fleece in harsh or cold winters.

**Harvest window:** March–October from successional sowings, typically about two months after sowing. Best eaten small, about 6in (15cm) tall, with the bulb no more than ½–1in (1–2.5cm) across; flavour sharpens as they grow.

**Storing:** A fresh, successional crop rather than a stored one; lift plants as needed.

**Common problems:** Onion downy mildew, onion white rot, onion fly, slugs and snails.

**Varieties:**

- **'Photon'** (recommended)
- **'White Lisbon' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Guardsman' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Apache' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Ishikura' AGM** (recommended)
- **Spring onion** — Spring onions are ordinary bulb onions pulled young, with small white or red bulbs about ¾–2 inches (2–5 cm) across and hollow green leaves that are entirely edible. The bulbs are sweet and mild because the usual onion gases have not yet built up, while the leaves are grassier and more pungent; slice them raw into salads and sandwiches, or grill, roast and stir them through soups and egg dishes. They are among the first alliums of the season and go especially well with other spring vegetables such as asparagus, peas and radishes.

### Shallots

**Sow:** Sets are the usual, easier method. Seed can be sown indoors in late winter (February on the calendar), five or six seeds per module or small pot, later thinned to three or four, so each cell forms a clump (each seed makes only one bulb). Outdoors in mid-spring (April–May) once the soil is drier and warming, thinly in drills ½in (1.5cm) deep, rows about 10in (25cm) apart; thin first to 2in (5cm), then to 4in (10cm). Closer spacing gives more, smaller bulbs and a heavier total crop.

**Plant out:** Sets from early to mid-spring (January–April on the calendar includes late-winter plantings); some varieties can go in during autumn or winter (October–December) and are less cold-sensitive. Avoid autumn and winter planting on heavy, waterlogged soil or the bulbs may rot. Seed-raised clumps go out in spring after hardening off; do not split the clumps.

**Spacing:** Sets and seed-raised clumps 6–8in (15–20cm) apart, rows 10–12in (25–30cm) apart, with the set tip just showing. Birds often pull sets out – check and replant, or cover with reused fleece or netting until rooted.

**Care:** Sunny, fertile, well-drained ground; damp soil invites rot and fungal disease, so use raised beds or pots if drainage is poor (they generally do poorly in growing bags). Fork in a bucket of fully rotted manure (never fresh) or garden compost per square yard (square metre), or use a no-dig mulch. Once rooted, water only in long early-summer droughts or in containers, and stop once bulbs have swollen in mid-summer; later watering of spring-planted crops can worsen storage. Avoid wetting the leaves. On poor soil, an organic nitrogen-rich feed in late winter helps autumn-planted shallots; pots can have a general organic liquid feed from spring to mid-summer. Weed carefully. Remove flower stems at once; any bulbs that tried to flower should be used first, as they store poorly.

**Harvest window:** July–August, when the leaves turn yellow and flop. Ease clusters up with a hand fork, avoid bruising, and separate into individual bulbs. Use at once or dry for storage.

**Storing:** They usually keep at least six to eight months. Dry them unsstacked on wire mesh or slatted trays in full sun for roughly a fortnight, or under glass or in an airy shed if the weather is wet, until the skins feel papery. Keep only sound bulbs, trimmed of dried leaf, in net bags or a single tray layer, somewhere light, cool, dry and airy – not in the dark, which encourages sprouting.

**Common problems:** Bolting, allium leaf miner, leek rust, onion downy mildew, onion neck rot, onion white rot, leek moth, onion fly, slugs and snails.

**In the kitchen:** Shallots are elongated, pointed bulbs under copper, gold or pink papery skin, splitting into garlic-like cloves — typically two or three in small types and three to six in larger ones — with off-white flesh flushed purple. Raw they are crisp, spicy and astringent; cooked they turn delicate, sweet and faintly like garlic, so they stand in for onion or garlic in vinaigrettes, Béarnaise, sautés, curries and roasts. The name is said to come from Ascalon on the Mediterranean, and crusaders are rumoured to have carried them into Europe in the 11th century; they have been grown in Asia for thousands of years.

**Varieties:**

- **'Camelot'** (recommended)
- **'Longor' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Matador' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Zebrune' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Meloine' AGM** (recommended)
- **Banana shallot** — Banana shallots, also called echalions, are large and torpedo-shaped, about 4–7 inches (10–18 cm) long, with tan to pink-grey skins that peel easily and mild, sweet white flesh that tastes of both onion and garlic without the usual sharpness. They cook quickly, so they are favoured for sauces (including Béarnaise in France), soups, stews and light caramelising, and they can also be used raw or pickled whole. The elongated types were developed in northern France and are still grown in Brittany and the Loire, as well as in Belgium and the eastern counties of Britain.

## Cucurbits and others

### Aubergines

**Sow:** Under cover, usually from February, because plants need heat and a long season. For a heated glasshouse or warm conservatory you can start in January; if plants will go outdoors, wait until early March, as they must not go out until after the last frost. Germinate at 70°F (21°C) or more, in a heated propagator or a warm indoor spot. Grow on in good light at 60–64°F (16–18°C), watering as needed. Once the first true leaves appear, move each seedling into its own 3in (7.5cm) pot or module. Calendar: sow January–March.

**Plant out:** Into a heated glasshouse in April; an unheated one in early May; outdoors in late May or early June after the last frost (calendar May–June). Harden off first. Plant at the same depth as in the pot and put canes in at once. Outdoors, use a very warm, sunny, sheltered spot, such as a south-facing wall or patio, and give cloche or plastic-free fleece cover for two weeks or so. Warm outdoor soil with cloches for two weeks beforehand, and enrich with garden compost or well-rotted manure, or plant into a no-dig mulch.

**Spacing:** 2ft (60cm) apart in the ground. One plant to a pot at least 1ft (30cm) across of peat-free compost, or two to a standard growing bag.

**Care:** Keep moisture steady; pots may need daily watering in hot weather. In a glasshouse, mist the leaves with tepid water, ideally twice a day, to discourage red spider mite and help fruit set, or damp the floor on hot mornings to raise humidity. Mulch plants in the ground or a border after planting. In pots, give an organic high-potassium liquid feed every two weeks from first flower; plants in enriched ground may not need routine feed, though it can lift the yield. When plants are about 1ft (30cm) tall, pinch out the main tip to encourage side-shoots. Tie the main stem to a stout cane, and add extra canes for side-shoots once fruit weighs them down.

**Harvest window:** August–October, usually from August. Sever the stalk a short way above the fruit.

**Storing:** Cut for fresh use; no long store is given.

**Common problems:** Aphids, glasshouse red spider mite, glasshouse whitefly.

**Varieties:**

- **'Bonica' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Clara' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Galine' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Kaberi' AGM** (recommended)

### Celery

**Sow:** Start under cover, mid-March to early April, because seed needs warmth and the crop needs a long season. Sow thinly on the surface of moist peat-free seed mix (a light dusting of sieved compost is optional – the seed needs light to germinate) at 59°F (15°C) or more, in a heated propagator or under a clear bag. Once up, give plenty of light, water regularly, and keep above 50°F (10°C) or later bolting is more likely. Pot on into 3in (7.5cm) pots or modules when large enough (this may not be until several true leaves show); thin module sowings to one. If you cannot raise seedlings, buy plugs in late spring or early summer.

**Plant out:** After the last frost, usually late May or early June. Harden off. Young plants are cold-sensitive – delay if temperatures will fall below 50°F (10°C) for more than 12 hours until they are established. After planting, cover with cloches or biodegradable fleece to keep them warm and reduce later bolting, protect from slugs, and water regularly, preferably with stored rainwater.

**Spacing:** Plant in a block rather than a single row, about 9in (23cm) each way. Traditional trench celery goes in a single row, 12–18in (30–45cm) apart. The same 9in (23cm) spacing works in large pots, which will need generous watering all season. A traditional trench is 16–20in (40–50cm) wide and 12in (30cm) deep, refilled to 4in (10cm) below the rim with soil mixed with fully rotted manure or homemade compost; trench types can also be blanched with newspaper instead of soil.

**Care:** Prepare the site the autumn before if you can, or plant into a no-dig mulch. Steady moisture is essential – short of water, stems become stringy and too strongly flavoured, especially in warm, fast-growing weather. Pots dry quickly in summer. Use stored rainwater if you can, and water at the cooler ends of hot days. Mulch after planting. Prepared ground usually needs no extra feed; on poor soil, a light organic high-nitrogen dressing in summer once plants are established can help. In pots, a balanced liquid feed every two weeks through summer. Weed often – celery grows slowly and is easily swamped.

**Harvest window:** July–December. Self-blanching and green types from mid-summer to autumn, as soon as they reach a useful size and before leaves yellow, because older stems grow fibrous; most will not stand frost, so cover once nights cool and finish before the first frost. Traditional trench types from the latter part of autumn into early winter; shield them from severe frost with biodegradable fleece. Dig up whole plants as needed.

**Storing:** Lift as needed, with fleece protection for late plants; no indoor store is described.

**Common problems:** Bolting, celery leaf mining fly, slugs and snails.

**In the kitchen:** Celery grows as a cluster of thick, juicy, ribbed light-green stalks, typically 18–24 inches (46–61 cm) long, joined at a common base, with wide, parsley-like leaves. The stalks are firm, fibrous along the ribs, and juicy, good raw as a snack or in salads, or steamed, boiled, roasted and blanched. It is a Mediterranean plant, domesticated from bitter wild smallage once used mainly as a medicine.

**Varieties:**

- **'Victoria' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Lorreta'** (recommended)
- **'Granada'** (recommended)
- **'Tall Utah 52/70' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Tango' AGM** (recommended)

### Chard

**Sow:** The large seeds are easy to handle. Outdoors from early spring to mid-summer (March–August), 1in (2.5cm) deep and 4in (10cm) apart, rows 18in (45cm) apart, later thinned to 1ft (30cm), or to 2in (5cm) for mini-leaves. A July sowing gives leaves the following spring when growth restarts. For an earlier crop, sow one or two seeds per indoor module, covered with 1in (2.5cm) of compost, thinned to one, and move them out once they are well rooted and at least 2in (5cm) tall. In a pot at least 16in (40cm) wide and deep, sow from April to July, 4in (10cm) apart, covered with about 1in (2.5cm) of peat-free compost. Protect seedlings from slugs.

**Plant out:** When indoor or bought plants are at least 2in (5cm) tall (calendar highlights May), after hardening off. Move them with as little root disturbance as possible, water in, and protect from slugs. The same spacings apply in a pot at least 16in (40cm) wide and deep.

**Spacing:** 1ft (30cm) apart for full-sized plants; 2in (5cm) apart when grown for repeated baby salad cuttings.

**Care:** Rich, moisture-holding, free-draining soil, or a no-dig mulch. Water regularly until established; mature plants are fairly drought-tolerant but give a better leaf crop if watered in long dry spells. Pots need watering throughout. Mulch once plants are growing well (less practical around salad-leaf rows). Feed is seldom needed in reasonably fertile ground or fresh potting compost. Weed while plants are young. Cloches over winter can give a few leaves in mild spells and an earlier spring flush; pots can be moved into a glasshouse for light winter pickings.

**Harvest window:** March–November. Spring and mid-summer sowings together can supply most of the year. Leaves often reach at least 1ft (30cm). Take a few outer leaves, cutting the whole stalk at the base and leaving the centre to regrow in a few weeks, or, once plants are at least 8in (20cm) tall, cut the lot at least 2in (5cm) above the base and wait longer for a new flush. Several cuts are usual. Leaves up to about 2in (5cm) long can be eaten raw.

**Storing:** Used fresh as a standing leaf crop; no long store is described.

**Common problems:** Beet leaf miner, downy mildew, grey mould, slugs and snails.

**Varieties:**

- **'Rhubarb Chard' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Bright Lights' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Charlotte' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Fordhook Giant' AGM** (recommended)
- **'White Silver'** (recommended)
- **Green Swiss chard** — Green Swiss chard has broad, wavy, crinkled green leaves on snow-white stalks and veins, milder and more succulent than the coloured types — hence the nickname ‘butter chard’ — with a gently salty taste to the stems. Young leaves can be eaten raw; larger ones are better sautéed, braised, baked or grilled. Chard is a leaf-selected beet, descended from the wild sea beet of European, Middle Eastern and North African coasts.
- **Rainbow chard** — Rainbow chard is a mix of stems in gold, crimson, pink, orange, violet, ivory and green, topped with broad, textured leaves, earthy, slightly sweet, bitter and mineral in flavour. Use the tender young leaves raw and cook the rest as you would any chard. Like other chards it comes from the wild sea beet of Mediterranean and Atlantic shores.
- **Red chard** — Red chard has thick crimson stems and crinkled, deeply veined dark-green or bronze leaves, sometimes flushed purple, with an earthy, bitter, mineral flavour. Treat it as other chard: raw when young, cooked when mature. It shares the same sea-beet ancestry as the green and rainbow forms.

### Chilli pepper

**Sow:** Under cover from late winter to mid-spring (February–April); an early start lengthens the harvest. Germinate at about 70°F (21°C) in a heated propagator, or on a warm windowsill. Once seedlings appear, grow on at 60–64°F (16–18°C) in bright light, with the compost evenly moist. At about 1in (2–3cm) tall, move each seedling into a module or a small pot filled with peat-free compost, then into 5in (13cm) pots once roots show through the drainage holes.

**Plant out:** Hold plants in a warm place, generally through to the end of May or later. They crop best in a glasshouse, polytunnel, conservatory or cold frame; compact types can stay on a sunny indoor sill. Outdoors only in mild districts, once night temperatures stay at least 54°F (12°C), usually late May or early June, after two to three weeks of hardening off. Plant at the same depth as before and cane taller types. Warm outdoor soil under cloches for two weeks or so first, on fertile, well-drained, moisture-holding ground, or plant into a no-dig mulch.

**Spacing:** 15–18in (38–45cm) apart according to variety; dwarf types 1ft (30cm) apart. One plant to a 9in (22cm) pot of peat-free compost, or three to a standard growing bag.

**Care:** Nights of about 54°F (12°C) are tolerable, but growth is better above 59°F (15°C). Heat over 86°F (30°C) can reduce fruiting, so ventilate and shade a glasshouse in hot spells. They like humidity – damp the glasshouse floor daily in warm weather. Keep soil or compost evenly moist; pots may need daily watering in hot, dry weather. Mulch plants in the ground. In pots, give an organic high-potassium liquid feed weekly from first flower; plants in enriched ground may not need routine feed, though it can lift the yield. Pinching the tip at about 1ft (30cm) tall is not usually necessary but can give more, smaller fruits, at the cost of later cropping. Big specimens, and any grown in bags, often need several canes; stems can be brittle, so tie them in gently. Plants kept over winter should be cut back to a manageable size, held in a warm, bright place, and watered sparingly.

**Harvest window:** July–October. Under glass, picking usually runs from mid-summer well into autumn.

**Storing:** Fruits can be dried, frozen or pickled for winter use.

**Common problems:** Aphids, grey mould, glasshouse red spider mite, glasshouse whitefly.

**Varieties:**

- **'Apache' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Fuego' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Hot Thai' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Hungarian Hot Wax' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Loco' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Prairie Fire' AGM** (recommended)
- **Cayenne** — Cayenne is a slender, tapering chilli, typically 4–6 inches (10–15 cm) long and ⅜–1 inch (1–3 cm) across, ripening from pale green to a glossy bright red, with thin, crisp flesh. Use it fresh (seeds and ribs removed if you want to moderate the heat) or sauté, roast and bake it. It is native to French Guiana on the north-east coast of South America and later spread through the Caribbean and the wider Americas.
- **Jalapeño** — Jalapeños are curved to straight conical pods, typically 2–6 inches (5–15 cm) long and ¾–2 inches (2–5 cm) across, picked dark green or left to redden, with thick, crisp, juicy flesh. They are one of the most widely used cooking chillies, raw or roasted, baked, grilled and simmered. The type takes its name from Xalapa, capital of Veracruz in Mexico, Iberian travellers later took it east and west during the 1400s and 1500s.
- **Padrón** — Padrón peppers are small, furrowed, conical pods, typically ¾–3 inches (2–7 cm) long, shiny green ripening to red, with semi-thick crisp flesh. They can be eaten raw, but are classically fried or grilled. The type descends from American peppers brought to Spain in the 16th century; in the 18th century Franciscans began growing them in Spanish regions, and they are now closely associated with the town of Padrón.
- **Shishito** — Shishitos are slender, slightly wrinkled Japanese peppers, typically 2–3 inches (5–7 cm) long, with a folded tip, thin crisp flesh and a mild, peppery flavour that deepens when the pods are blistered, grilled or sautéed. They are thought to have been selected in Japan from Padrón-type peppers introduced by the Portuguese in the 16th century.
- **Orange habanero** — Orange habaneros are small, lantern-shaped, deeply wrinkled pods, typically 2–3 inches (5–7 cm) long and ¾–2 inches (2–5 cm) across, ripening from green to a vivid orange, with thin, crisp flesh. They are used raw or cooked in salsas, hot sauces and marinades, and for roasting or sautéing. The type descends from peppers found in South American rainforests more than 8,000 years ago and later carried into Central America, Mexico and the Caribbean.
- **Scotch Bonnet** — Scotch Bonnets are slightly flattened, squat, four- or five-lobed chillies, typically 2–5 inches (5–12 cm) long and 2–3 inches (5–8 cm) across, with thin, waxy skin that ripens from green to red and crisp, juicy flesh. They flavour hot sauces, chutneys and Caribbean cooking, raw or roasted, boiled or sautéed. They descend from Amazon Basin peppers later grown in the West Indies.
- **Thai red chilli** — Thai red chillies vary in shape but are generally small and slender, about ¾–3 inches (2–7 cm) long, ripening from green to a bright or dark red, with a fiery, slightly sweet, earthy heat. Wear gloves when you handle them, and keep your hands away from your eyes. They descend from South and Central American peppers that later became central to South-East Asian cooking.

### Courgettes

**Sow:** Indoors, one seed on its side, ½in (13mm) deep, in a 3in (7.5cm) pot or module of moist peat-free seed mix, germinated at 65–70°F (18–21°C). Outdoors, two or three seeds 1in (2.5cm) deep at stations at least 3ft (90cm) apart, watered and covered with a cloche or plastic-free fleece, left on as long as possible after they come up. Calendar: sow April–June.

**Plant out:** Early summer once the soil has warmed (May–June), or buy plants towards the end of spring and the start of summer. Harden them off, set each plant in the centre of a prepared station at least 3ft (90cm) apart, firm gently and water. Compact or bush types can go in a pot at least 18in (45cm) wide, or one or even two compact plants in a growing bag, watered regularly.

**Spacing:** At least 3ft (90cm) apart, in a warm, sunny site, or planted into a no-dig mulch at each station.

**Care:** These plants drink a lot and should be watered often, especially in pots and while fruiting; pots may need daily watering in hot weather. Water at the base, not over the leaves, to limit fungal trouble such as powdery mildew. A 6in (15cm) pot sunk beside the plant makes a useful watering well. Use stored rainwater if you can, and water at the cooler ends of hot days. Mulch thickly. In pots, give a high-potash liquid feed every 10–14 days once the first fruits start to swell; open-ground plants on reasonably fertile soil usually need no feed. Remove tatty or mildewed leaves.

**Harvest window:** July–October. Cut fruits small, tender and flavoursome, at 4–5in (10–12.5cm) long, with a knife or secateurs so the plant is not torn.

**Storing:** Best picked small and fresh; no long store is described (unlike marrows and pumpkins).

**Common problems:** Courgette, marrow, pumpkin and squash problems; downy mildew; grey mould.

**In the kitchen:** The green courgette, or zucchini, is a straight, almost untapered cylinder, at its best at about 5–6 inches (13–15 cm) and often picked up to 6–8 inches (15–20 cm). The glossy dark-green skin and mild flesh work in both savoury and sweet cooking — grated into breads and fritters, or sliced and cooked as a vegetable. Though many summer squashes come from the Americas, this type is thought to be a Milanese selection, listed in an Italian seed pamphlet of 1901.

**Varieties:**

- **'Parador' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Defender' AGM** (recommended)
- **'El Greco' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Lorea' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Tuscany' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Astia' AGM** (recommended)
- **Goldmine courgette** — Goldmine is a slim yellow courgette, brightest and most tender at about 5½ inches (14 cm) or less, with shiny gold skin marked by thin white stripes and green at the stem and blossom ends. Use it exactly as you would a green courgette, raw or cooked; the skin is delicate enough to leave on. Golden courgettes were first released in the 1970s by Burpee, using seed from Dr Oved Shifriss, who identified the bi-colour ‘B’ gene behind green-and-yellow fruit.
- **Yellow patty pan** — Baby yellow patty pan is a small, saucer-shaped summer squash, about 1½–3 inches (4–8 cm) across, with a scalloped, petal-like rim, shiny yellow skin and a green-tipped stem. The skin is soft enough to eat raw, and the little fruits also sauté, bake, braise or steam well, or go into salads and platters. The type is a New World squash, eaten by Native Americans since at least the late 16th century.
- **Tromboncino** — Tromboncino is a long, slender-necked squash, typically 2–3 feet (60–91 cm) long, straight or curved, with a bulbous end. Picked early it has thin, pale-green, faintly striped skin and can be eaten raw or cooked like a courgette; left on the vine the skin hardens to tan and it stores as a winter squash. Steam, grill, sauté, bake or roast it, or slice it into salads and sandwiches. It comes from Liguria on the north-west Italian coast, though the wider squash family is Central and South American.
- **Costata Romanesco** — Costata Romanesco is an Italian cocozelle-type courgette, ribbed from stem to blossom and striped dark and light green with a pale speckled overlay, rather than the usual smooth zucchini skin. It is prized for flavour and texture, excellent raw in thin ribbons or rounds, or cooked in the usual ways. Cocozelle types have been grown in Italy since the Renaissance and are thought to have originated around Rome, later spreading north of the Mediterranean.

### Cucumbers

**Sow:** Best started under cover so fruiting begins sooner; move plants to their final spot after roughly four weeks, once growth is vigorous. Timing: mid-February to mid-March for a heated glasshouse; April for an unheated glasshouse, polytunnel or large cold frame; late April for outdoor crops. Sow one seed ½–¾in (1–2cm) deep in the centre of a 4in (10cm) pot of peat-free seed mix, at 70°F (21°C) in a heated propagator or under a clear bag on a warm sunny sill, then keep warm, bright and watered. Outdoor varieties can also be sown in the final spot at the end of May or the start of June, particularly in milder southern districts or during a warm spell: up to three seeds together, ½–¾in (1–2cm) deep, then covered with cloches or plastic-free fleece, left on after germination.

**Plant out:** Late spring or early summer (May–June). Under glass from late May once the indoor temperature stays above 53°F (12°C), into a border or large pots. Outdoors from early June, after careful hardening, once local temperatures stay reliably above 53°F (12°C). Water before moving and keep the rootball intact. Put supports in at planting if you will train plants upwards.

**Spacing:** In the ground, 1ft (30cm) between plants and between prepared stations (each station gets two buckets of garden compost or well-rotted manure, or a no-dig mulch). One plant to a pot at least 1ft (30cm) wide and deep, or two to a standard growing bag. Glasshouse plants can climb a tall cane to the roof, or a vertical wire or string fixed at ground level.

**Care:** Warmth and even moisture are essential. Water glasshouse and pot plants regularly, especially while fruit is forming; pots may need daily watering in hot spells. Outdoor plants in the ground need less frequent attention if mulched, but still water in dry weather. Water at the base, not over the leaves (mildew risk), preferably in the morning with stored rainwater. A pot sunk beside each plant sends water to the roots and keeps it off the stem neck, which can rot. Mulch thickly. Open-ground plants rarely need feed unless the soil is very poor; pots get a general liquid feed every 10–14 days.

**Harvest window:** July–October. Under glass, from mid-summer to mid-autumn; a shorter outdoor season depending on the weather. As a guide, pick mini fruits at about 3in (8cm), small-fruited types at 6in (15cm), and full-sized types at 8–10in (20–25cm). Check every few days, as fruits swell quickly. Pick just before eating for the best flavour and crunch.

**Storing:** Best used promptly; long storage is not described.

**Common problems:** Cucumber mosaic virus, glasshouse red spider mite, glasshouse whitefly, powdery mildew, slugs and snails.

**In the kitchen:** The common cucumber is usually a long, tapered cylinder, about 4–12 inches (10–30 cm) long, with thick, shiny dark-green skin, though some types are short and round, ribbed or paler. Most are eaten raw in salads or pickled. The plant comes from south and central Asia — the northern plains of India, more than three millennia ago — and arrived in Europe with the Romans.

**Varieties:**

- **'Carmen' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Emilie' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Lili' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Marketmore' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Mini Munch' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Delistar' AGM** (recommended)
- **Greenhouse (hothouse) cucumber** — Hothouse cucumbers are long, slim fruits, typically 10–15 inches (25–38 cm) long and 1–2 inches (2.5–5 cm) across, with thin, dark-green, slightly ribbed skin that needs no peeling. Slice them raw, or grill, purée, pickle or briefly cook them in soups. Cucumbers reached England in the 14th century and grew in popularity over the following three hundred years; these long, thin-skinned types are the ones grown under glass.
- **Lemon cucumber** — Lemon cucumbers are round, tennis-ball fruits about 2–3 inches (5–7 cm) across, with thin, tender skin that ripens from lemon to gold, sometimes slightly bristly, and pale, juicy flesh. They are usually eaten raw — some people peel them if the skin tastes bitter — in salads or as a garnish. The type likely comes from the Middle East or India and first appeared in Samuel Wilson’s American catalogue in 1894.
- **Gherkin** — Gherkins are small, bumpy cucumbers, typically 1½–3 inches (4–8 cm) long, with firm green skin and crisp, juicy pale flesh and a few edible seeds. They can be eaten fresh with dips or quickly sautéed, though they are best known as a pickling cucumber. They are thought to have originated in India and spread early into Europe and the Middle East.
- **Cornichon** — Cornichons are very small, narrow cucumbers, about ¾–3 inches (2–7 cm) long, often slightly spiny, with crisp, watery pale-green flesh that may be almost seedless. They can be eaten fresh, but they are classically salted overnight and pickled in hot vinegar. Native to India, they entered French cooking in the 1700s and were widely grown in local gardens, giving rise to many French strains.
- **Armenian cucumber** — Despite the name, the Armenian cucumber is botanically a melon, a slender, often curled fruit about 10–15 inches (25–38 cm) long, with thin, ribbed, pale to dark-green skin that is slightly fuzzy when young. It is mild enough to eat skin-and-all, raw, grilled, puréed or pickled. It was first selected in Armenia in the 15th century, from a crop with tropical and subtropical Asian origins.
- **Pickling cucumber** — Pickling cucumbers are short, firm cylinders, typically 2–4 inches (5–10 cm) long, with thin pale-green skin, lighter stripes and a few knobs, and flesh that stays crisp even after brining. They are usually preserved in vinegar, salt and spice; the blossom end should be trimmed off first, as it can soften the pickle. The practice is ancient — cucumbers were recorded in the Tigris valley of India in 2030 BCE — and industrial pickling later made them a worldwide staple.

### Endive

**Sow:** Seed needs a soil temperature of at least 59°F (15°C), ideally 68–72°F (20–22°C). Sow frisée and Batavian types outdoors from late spring, with cloches or plastic-free fleece if needed, or start under cover, especially in colder districts (calendar February–August). For winter heads, sow Batavian/escarole types in mid- to late August, covering from autumn. Under cover, sow ½in (1cm) deep in moist peat-free seed mix on a warm sunny sill or in a heated propagator, then pot on once seedlings have several leaves. Early sowings bolt if kept below 41°F (5°C) for too long – use bolt-resistant varieties and keep them indoors well into spring. Late-summer Batavian sowings can also go in a glasshouse border or large pot; thin to 9–13in (23–38cm), or 2in (5cm) for baby leaves. Outdoors from late May once the soil is at least 59°F (15°C), through August, in ½in (1cm) drills 1ft (30cm) apart.

**Plant out:** Once local temperatures stay well above 41°F (5°C) – perhaps from late April in warmer southern districts, but not until early June further north (calendar April–June). Harden off, water before and after planting. August-sown young plants can go out under cloches or into a glasshouse border or large pot.

**Spacing:** 9–13in (23–38cm) for heading plants; 2in (5cm) for baby salad leaves. A pot 12–16in (30–40cm) wide of peat-free compost is suitable.

**Care:** Sun, with partial shade acceptable in summer. Most soils suit it if they neither dry out badly nor lie wet; or use a no-dig mulch. Water regularly, especially on free-draining ground and in warm weather, and never let pots dry in summer. Weed to reduce competition for water. Mulch between rows once plants are growing well. Feed is seldom needed, but on light soils or in pots a general organic liquid feed every two weeks in summer can help. For a winter crop, choose sturdier Batavian or escarole types and cover outdoor plants from October with a cloche or a plastic-free fleece sheet.

**Harvest window:** March–November. With regular sowings you can take repeated leaf cuttings from spring to autumn, or mature heads from summer into winter. Frisée types are for summer; sturdier Batavian/escarole types can also stand into winter.

**Storing:** Used fresh as leaves or heads; no long store is described.

**Common problems:** Bolting, aphids, slugs and snails.

**Varieties:**

- **'Pancalieri' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Natacha' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Wallone' AGM** (recommended)
- **Curly endive** — Curly endive forms a loose head of narrow, lacy, green-tipped outer leaves around a compact off-white heart; the darker leaves are distinctly bitter, the paler heart milder and slightly sweet. It is mostly used raw in salads, though it can be cooked. Endive is thought to have arisen around Sicily and the Mediterranean, was a salad green in Egypt, Rome and Greece, and had reached England by 1548.
- **Escarole** — Escarole grows as a flattened rosette, typically 12–22 inches (30–55 cm) across, of broad, wavy leaves with a thick midrib, mildly bitter and green in flavour (bitterness varies with growing conditions). Use it raw or cooked. It descends from wild chicories of the eastern Mediterranean and Asia.

### Florence fennel

**Sow:** The plants resent root disturbance, so sow direct outdoors or one seed to a module under cover (April–July). Outdoors, thinly in a ½in (15mm) drill, rows 1ft (30cm) apart, then thin gradually to 1ft (30cm), taking care not to loosen the remaining roots. Keep indoor seedlings brightly lit and water them often.

**Plant out:** From early May after the last frost (May–June). Harden off, plant at the same depth as before without disturbing the roots, into prepared ground or large pots of peat-free compost, and water in.

**Spacing:** 1ft (30cm) apart, or 4–6in (10–15cm) if you will pick them as baby bulbs.

**Care:** A warm, sheltered place in sun, on fertile ground that drains freely – it does especially well on ground improved for a preceding spring crop, and dislikes poorly drained or waterlogged ground. Use a no-dig mulch if that is your method, or grow compact types in large pots if the soil is unsuitable. Keep the soil or compost evenly moist so growth stays even. Mulch once plants are growing well, and keep weed-free.

**Harvest window:** July–November, in late summer and autumn according to sowing date. Bulbs are usually cut at 4–6in (10–15cm) across, but can be taken from about 2in (5cm) upwards. Slice the bulb off at the base and trim away the feathery tops.

**Storing:** Cut for fresh use; no long store is given.

**Common problems:** Bolting, slugs and snails.

**In the kitchen:** Florence fennel forms a pale, swollen, layered ‘bulb’ of thick, ribbed stems topped with feathery leaves. Almost every part is edible — bulb, stems, fronds, flowers and seed — with a mild, green, aniseed flavour, used raw or cooked. It is a southern European, especially Mediterranean, plant that has grown wild since antiquity.

**Varieties:**

- **'Dragon' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Rondo' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Zefa Fino'** (recommended)

### Lettuce

**Sow:** Indoors or out, in pots or in the ground, from spring into autumn (calendar February–October). Useful as a catch-crop between slower plants such as parsnips and sweetcorn. For a very early crop, start under cover in early February and move plants out in early March with cloche cover. For early-winter heads, sow outdoors in early August and cover from late September, or grow in a glasshouse border or pot. For spring heads, sow a winter type such as 'Winter Density' in September or October, in a glasshouse, or in mild districts under cloches or in a cold frame. In hot weather some varieties germinate poorly – sow after the heat of the day, use cold water, and give some shade. Under cover, sow thinly in moist peat-free seed mix in a warm bright place, and pot on once seedlings have a few leaves.

**Plant out:** From spring onwards (calendar March–April highlighted), after hardening off, into a warm sunny bed or a pot at least 1ft (30cm) wide and deep of peat-free compost. In spring and in colder districts, cloches or plastic-free fleece help plants settle. In hot weather, use light shade and water often, to limit wilting and bolting.

**Spacing:** About 1ft (30cm) for larger hearting types – closer planting spoils the hearts – and about 6in (15cm) for smaller loose-leaf types.

**Care:** Fertile, moisture-holding soil, or a no-dig mulch. Keep the soil evenly moist, especially for seedlings and new transplants in warm, dry weather – drought can cause bolting. Pots dry fast: keep them out of midday sun and expect daily watering in summer. Water early in the morning, not in the evening, which can draw slugs and encourage grey mould. Mulch once plants are growing well.

**Harvest window:** May–November, and into winter with the right sowings and varieties. Loose-leaf types can be ready after only four to six weeks in warm weather; hearting types take about 10–14 weeks. Pick loose-leaf as cut-and-come-again once leaves are about 4in (10cm) long, either a few from each plant or all the top growth, leaving a short stem that should resprout at least once if it is not too hot or dry. Cut whole hearts when they feel firm, leaving a stump about 1in (2.5cm) tall. In hot weather, put picked leaves straight into water so they do not wilt.

**Storing:** Best used promptly; long storage is not described.

**Common problems:** Bolting, aphids, grey mould, cutworms, lettuce downy mildew, root aphids, slugs and snails.

**Varieties:**

The source page says little about cooking, but the small hearts are a natural for salads and for serving whole.

### Marrow

**Sow:** Best started under cover from mid- to late April for a longer season and more reliable results in colder districts, safe from slugs. Sow one seed on its side, ½in (1.5cm) deep, in a 3in (7.5cm) pot or module of moist peat-free seed mix, at 65–70°F (18–21°C). Keep plants warm, bright and watered until after the last frost. They can also be sown outdoors after frost has finished. Calendar: sow April–June.

**Plant out:** Early summer once the soil has warmed (May–June), or buy plants towards the end of spring and the start of summer. Harden them off, set each plant in the centre of a prepared station without disturbing the roots, firm, water, and protect from slugs. Compact or bush types can go in a large pot, though they generally crop better in the open ground.

**Spacing:** At least 3ft (90cm) apart for bush types and 5ft (1.5m) for trailing types, in a warm, sunny site, or planted into a no-dig mulch at each station.

**Care:** Water regularly and generously, especially while flowering and fruiting, and in pots – daily in hot spells. Water at the cooler ends of the day, with stored rainwater if you can, and keep water off the leaves to limit powdery mildew. A 6in (15cm) pot sunk beside the plant makes a useful watering well. Mulch thickly. Remove tatty or mildewed leaves, which also helps the skins ripen in the sun.

**Harvest window:** July–October, from mid-summer, at 8–12in (20–30cm) long for immediate use. Regular cutting encourages more fruit, which can swell quickly in warm, damp weather. Take the last fruits before frost.

**Storing:** Leave fruits for storage to finish ripening in sunshine until the rind feels firm, but bring them in well before frost. Keep them cool, dark and frost-free, such as in a garage, where they should last a month or two.

**Common problems:** Courgette, marrow, pumpkin and squash problems; grey mould.

**Varieties:**

- **'Badger Cross' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Bush Baby'** (recommended)
- **'Tiger Cross' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Long Green Trailing'** (recommended)

### Okra

**Sow:** Under cover in March or April. Soak the seed for two hours in warm water, then sow individually in small pots or modules of moist peat-free compost, and keep above 60°F (16°C) in a lidded propagator or under a clear bag. Keep warm, water regularly, and pot on as they grow.

**Plant out:** In late May or June, once plants are 4–6in (10–15cm) tall. In the UK they are best kept in a glasshouse, in large pots, growing bags or a border; only try outdoors in really warm spots. Plant at the same depth as before.

**Spacing:** One plant to a pot at least 1ft (30cm) wide and deep of peat-free compost, or two to a standard growing bag. In a glasshouse border, improve the soil with homemade compost or fully rotted manure and set plants 1–3ft (30–90cm) apart according to the variety (check the packet or label).

**Care:** They need plenty of sun and high summer temperatures, and a steady supply of moisture, especially in pots. Use tepid water if you can, preferably from a butt, in the early morning or late afternoon. Mulch plants in the ground once they are growing strongly. Enriched open ground usually needs no extra feed; in pots, start a weekly organic high-potassium liquid feed at first flower.

**Harvest window:** July–October, from mid-summer until the weather cools in autumn, often right up to the first frost. Pick pods while tender and immature, at about 2–4in (5–10cm) long – larger, mature pods turn tough and stringy. Cut the thin stalk with secateurs or a sharp knife.

**Storing:** Young pods are picked for fresh use; no long store is given.

**Common problems:** Aphids, glasshouse red spider mite, glasshouse whitefly, powdery mildew.

### Peppers

**Sow:** Under cover, mid-February to the start of March for plants that will stay under glass, and late March or the beginning of April for outdoor crops (calendar February–April). Germinate at 65–70°F (18–21°C) in a heated propagator or on a warm windowsill. Once seedlings appear, grow on at 60–64°F (16–18°C) in bright light, with the compost evenly moist. When they have two true leaves, move each into a 3–3½in (7.5–9cm) pot and keep the temperature up so growth stays strong.

**Plant out:** Hold plants in a warm place, generally through to the end of May or later (calendar April–June). They crop best in a glasshouse, polytunnel, conservatory or cold frame. Outdoors only in very warm, sheltered spots once night temperatures stay above 54°F (12°C), after a couple of weeks of hardening off; outdoor plants usually set fewer fruits, and the shorter season can leave fruit unripe as nights cool in early autumn. Give them the warmest, sunniest place, ideally a patio or south-facing wall, at 59°F (15°C) or more. Plant at the same depth as before and cane taller types.

**Spacing:** 15–18in (38–45cm) apart according to variety; compact types 1ft (30cm) apart. One plant in the centre of a pot at least 1ft (30cm) wide, filled with peat-free compost. Support each plant with a stout cane, tying in the main stem, and add more canes and soft twine for heavy, brittle side-shoots. Plants usually reach only about 2ft (60cm) tall and 1ft (30cm) wide, and do well in large pots.

**Care:** Nights of 54°F (12°C) are tolerable, but growth is better above 59°F (15°C). Heat over 86°F (30°C) can reduce fruiting, so ventilate and shade a glasshouse in summer. They like high humidity – damp the glasshouse floor regularly in warm weather. Keep soil or compost evenly moist; pots may need daily watering in hot, dry weather. Mulch plants in the ground. In pots, give an organic high-potassium liquid feed weekly from first flower. Pinching the tip at about 1ft (30cm) tall is not usually necessary but can give more, smaller fruits, at the cost of later cropping.

**Harvest window:** July–October. Under glass, from mid-summer into autumn. Pick regularly to encourage more fruit. As the season closes, give outdoor plants plastic-free fleece cover, or move potted specimens under cover, so leftover fruits can colour up.

**Storing:** Picked fresh, with late fruit left to ripen; no long store is described.

**Common problems:** Blossom end rot, aphids, grey mould, glasshouse red spider mite, glasshouse whitefly.

### Pumpkins

**Sow:** Best started under cover from mid- to late April for surer germination and a longer season; outdoor sowing can follow about a month later. Sow one flat seed on its side, ½in (1cm) deep, in a 3in (7.5cm) pot or module of peat-free compost, at 65–70°F (18–21°C) in a heated propagator or under a clear bag on a warm bright sill. Water regularly, keep in good light, and pot on once roots fill the container. Calendar: sow April–May.

**Plant out:** After the last frost (May–June), after hardening off. Firm gently and water. Very compact bush types can go one to a growing bag, or one to a pot at least 18in (45cm) wide of peat-free or soil-based compost, in a sunny, open spot. Avoid crowding, which encourages fungal disease. Prepare each station as a 1ft (30cm) hole filled with a mix of soil and homemade compost, or plant into a no-dig mulch. For giant fruits, an organic general fertiliser can be used. Choose a warm, sunny, wind-sheltered site with fertile, moisture-holding soil that does not lie wet – they do less well in cool spots.

**Spacing:** Bush types 3ft (90cm) apart; trailing types 5ft (1.5m) apart. Even compact bush plants can easily reach 3ft (90cm) across.

**Care:** Water generously, especially while fruiting. A 6in (15cm) pot sunk beside each plant sends water to the roots; a short cane marks the crown once the vines sprawl. Short of water, fruiting suffers and powdery mildew is more likely. Use stored rainwater if you can, and water at the cooler ends of hot days. If space is tight, train trailing stems in a wide circle, pegged with short canes, or up a stout arch, large wigwam or strong trellis, especially for smaller fruits, which may need individual net slings. Larger-fruited types are better left on the ground.

**Harvest window:** September–October. Leave fruits on the plant as long as you can, but cut them before the first frost, or protect with straw, fleece or cardboard – frosted fruits will not store. Cut with at least 4in (10cm) of stem attached. Cure the skins in sunshine for about 7–10 days, outdoors or in a glasshouse or cold frame.

**Storing:** Keep fully ripened fruits, not touching, in a well-ventilated place at 50–60°F (10–15°C), for winter use.

**Common problems:** Courgette, marrow, pumpkin and squash problems; grey mould; slugs and snails.

**Varieties:**

- **'Hundredweight'** (recommended)
- **'Charmant'** (recommended)
- **'Cargo'** (recommended)
- **'Atlantic Giant'** (recommended)
- **'Jack be Little'** (recommended)

### Salad leaves

**Sow:** In the ground or in pots from spring to autumn (February–September). Useful as a catch-crop between slower plants such as parsnips and potatoes. Under cover, scatter seed thinly, cover lightly with fine compost, pot on once seedlings have a few leaves, and move them out after a few more weeks, from spring to late summer, once they are growing strongly. Autumn sowings can overwinter in a glasshouse or on a sunny sill, or, in mild districts, hardy types under cloches or in a cold frame. Outdoors, sow in short ½in (1cm) drills 12in (30cm) apart, or broadcast over a small patch and cover lightly.

**Plant out:** From spring, once indoor or bought plants are about 4in (10cm) tall, after hardening off, into prepared ground or a pot at least 1ft (30cm) wide and deep of peat-free compost. Autumn-sown plants can go in a glasshouse border for winter, or, in mild districts, hardy types under cloches or in a cold frame.

**Spacing:** 4–6in (10–15cm) apart, according to the variety – check the packet or label.

**Common problems:** Bolting, aphids, brassica downy mildew, damping off, cabbage whitefly, grey mould, cutworms, lettuce downy mildew, flea beetles, root aphids, slugs and snails.

### Spinach

**Sow:** Usually best sown direct, in the ground or in pots (calendar March–May and August–September). Sow plenty if you will cook it, as leaves shrink a lot. Useful as a fast gap-filler. Module sowing, one seed per cell, reduces root disturbance and can give an earlier crop. Do not sow too early in the cold, or use cloches or plastic-free fleece – early plants often bolt. For late summer to early autumn, and for autumn or early-spring pickings, use hardy varieties. Sow thinly in a 1in (2.5cm) drill, rows 8in (20cm) apart.

**Plant out:** Module-raised or bought plants can go out from mid-spring once they are about 4in (10cm) tall, after hardening off, with as little root disturbance as possible. Firm gently and water. A pot at least 1ft (30cm) wide and deep of peat-free compost, in sun or light shade, also works. Autumn-sown plants can go in a glasshouse border for winter, or, in mild districts, hardy types under cloches or in generous pots inside a cold frame.

**Spacing:** 6in (15cm) apart at least – crowding can encourage downy mildew.

**Care:** Fertile, moisture-holding soil; on poor ground, rake in an organic general fertiliser, or sow into a no-dig mulch. Water in dry, warm weather – drought checks growth and causes bolting – especially in pots. Water early in the morning, not in the evening, which can draw slugs and downy mildew. Growing through the cooler, damper months of spring and autumn, and in the ground rather than in pots, reduces the need to water. Weed, especially while plants are young, and protect from slugs.

**Harvest window:** March–November. Baby leaves can be ready in about a month, mature leaves in about two. Pick as cut-and-come-again: a few leaves from each plant every few weeks until they start to flower, or, once plants have a good root system after two months or so, cut the lot; well-rooted plants should give at least one more flush. Young leaves are good raw; larger ones are best cooked only briefly.

**Storing:** Used fresh; no long store is described.

**Common problems:** Bolting, downy mildew, pigeons, slugs and snails.

**In the kitchen:** Spinach is a low-growing rosette of flat or slightly curved leaves in many shapes, from oval and arrowhead to deeply lobed, with a mild, earthy, slightly mineral taste, used raw or cooked according to type and age. It descends from wild greens of ancient Persia, in what is now Iran, later selected into the garden crop.

### Squash

**Sow:** Usually started under cover from mid- to late April for surer germination, a longer season and protection from late frost, then moved out after the last frost. Sow one seed on its side, ½in (13mm) deep, in a 3in (7.5cm) pot or module of peat-free seed or multi-purpose mix, at 65–70°F (18–21°C) with gentle heat or on a warm indoor sill. Water regularly and keep in good light. Calendar: sow April–May.

**Plant out:** After the last frost (May–June), after hardening off. Firm gently and water. Compact types can go one to a pot at least 18in (45cm) wide of peat-free or soil-based compost. Avoid crowding, which encourages fungal disease. Prepare each station as a 1ft (30cm) hole filled with a mix of soil and homemade garden compost, or plant into a no-dig mulch, on a warm, sunny site with rich, moisture-holding soil that does not lie wet.

**Spacing:** Bush types at least 3ft (90cm) apart; trailing types 5ft (1.5m). Even compact bush plants can easily spread to 3ft (90cm) across.

**Care:** Water regularly in dry spells, especially while fruiting – drought also encourages powdery mildew. Water the soil, not the leaves; a pot sunk beside each plant helps, and a short cane marks the crown of trailing types once the vines sprawl. Use stored rainwater if you can, and water at the cooler ends of the day. Trailing stems can sprawl, be trained in a wide circle pegged with short canes, or climb a stout arch, pergola, hazel wigwam or strong trellis, with net slings for heavy fruits; smaller-fruited types are more suitable for training up.

**Harvest window:** July–October. Summer types fruit for several months from mid-summer, sometimes until the first frost. Leave winter types on the plant as long as possible for flavour, but cut them before frost, with at least 4in (10cm) of stem – a cut too close to the fruit leaves a soft patch that rots in store. Bring everything in before frost risk.

**Storing:** For winter types, let fruits ripen fully on the plant, then cure the skins in the sun for another week or two, outdoors or in a glasshouse, and get them under cover before the first frost. Store in a well-ventilated place at 50–60°F (10–15°C), spaced so they do not touch, where they can keep for many months.

### Sweetcorn

**Sow:** Best started under cover in spring, especially in colder districts, because seed needs warmth. From mid- to late spring, sow 1in (2.5cm) deep, one seed to a deep pot or module of peat-free seed mix, at 65–70°F (18–21°C) on a warm sill or in a heated propagator; they usually appear within two weeks. Sow early, mid-season and late types together, or stagger batches of one early variety a few weeks apart, to spread the harvest. Calendar: sow April–June.

**Plant out:** Late May or early June, after the last frost, once plants are 3–6in (8–15cm) tall (at least 3in / 8cm). Harden off these tender plants carefully. Plant in a block or group, not a long row, so each plant has neighbours on all sides for wind pollination; a single row often gives poorly filled cobs with missing kernels.

**Spacing:** Plant in a close block for pollination rather than a long row; follow the packet for exact distances. A warm, sunny, wind-sheltered site with fertile soil, or a no-dig mulch.

**Care:** Water young plants in dry spells until they are established, and again in long droughts, especially at flowering and as cobs swell. Mulch thickly. Plants can reach 6½ft (2m) and rock in wind, which loosens roots – cane individuals, or put tall stakes around the block and loop string around the growth. Extra soil around the stems allows additional anchoring roots to form.

**Harvest window:** August–October, usually from late summer according to variety. Once the silks begin to brown, peel back a little husk and pierce a kernel: watery juice means it is not ready, creamy juice means it is ripe, and a pasty texture means it is past its best. Eat as soon as you can after picking, while the sweetness is at its peak. For popcorn, leave cobs on the plant until over-ripe and starting to dry.

**Storing:** Eat promptly for sweetness; popcorn cobs are left to dry on the plant. No other store is described.

**Common problems:** Grey squirrels, smuts, mice and voles, slugs and snails.

**Varieties:**

- **'Ovation' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Swift' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Mirai Picnic' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Lark' AGM** (recommended)

### Tomatoes

**Sow:** Under cover: late February to mid-March for a glasshouse crop, late March to early April for outdoor plants (calendar February–April). Germinate at about 64°F (18°C) in a heated propagator, or on a warm windowsill under a clear bag. Uncover once seedlings appear, usually within a fortnight, and give as much light as possible so they do not grow thin and leggy. When they have a couple of true leaves, move each into its own pot or module of peat-free compost, handling a leaf not the stem; if a seedling is leggy, set it deeper so the first leaves sit just above the compost.

**Plant out:** Early summer (May–June) once temperatures stay reliably above 60°F (16°C), into large pots or growing bags under glass or outdoors, or into the ground in a really warm, sunny, sheltered spot. Harden off first. Plant deeply so the first pair of leaves sits just above the soil – the buried stem will make extra roots. Do not put tomatoes back on the same ground two years running.

**Spacing:** 18–24in (45–60cm) apart according to eventual size – check the packet. One plant to a 12–18in (30–45cm) pot, or two to a standard growing bag. Bush types suit large patio pots, troughs, windowboxes and even hanging baskets; cordons need a tall cane in a large pot.

**Care:** Keep soil or compost evenly moist; pots may need daily watering in hot weather, and curling leaves can signal drought. A 6in (15cm) pot sunk beside the plant makes a useful watering well. In pots, give an organic high-potassium liquid feed every 10–14 days once the first fruits start to swell; plants in enriched ground may not need feed, though a fortnightly dose can lift the yield. Mulch plants in the ground or a glasshouse border. Cordon (indeterminate) types are grown as a single stem: pinch out side-shoots from the leaf joints each time you water, and tie the stem to a tall cane or wind it up a slightly slack vertical string. When the plant reaches the top of its support, or has set seven trusses under glass or four outdoors, stop the main tip two leaves above the top truss. Bush types keep their side-shoots; cane any heavy, drooping shoots. Tomatoes do not cling on their own and must be tied in.

**Harvest window:** July–October, from mid-summer, according to variety, weather and fruit size. At the end of the season, lift outdoor plants that still have unripe fruit and lay them on straw under cloches, or pick the fruits and ripen them somewhere warm and dark.

**Storing:** Leftover green fruit can be ripened in warmth and darkness, or under cloches; no long store of ripe fruit is given.

**Common problems:** Tomato blight; fruit ripening problems; fruit splitting and cracking; leaf problems; stem problems; grey mould; glasshouse whitefly.

**Varieties:**

## Perennials

### Asparagus

**Sow:** The quickest start is bought bare-root crowns, but seed is possible. Indoors in February at 55–61°F (13–16°C), kept warm, bright and well watered, then hardened off for a move in early June. Outdoors, thinly in a 1in (2.5cm) drill, rows 12–18in (30–45cm) apart, thinned to 6in (15cm), and protected from slugs. Calendar: sow February–April. Seed-raised plants are planted like crowns once they are a year old.

**Plant out:** Plant crowns promptly on arrival, with March the preferred month. Open a hole about 1ft (30cm) wide and 8in (20cm) deep, mound 4in (10cm) of soil in the centre, set the crown on the mound with buds or new shoots upwards, spread the roots, water to settle, and mulch with 2in (5cm) of well-rotted manure or garden compost.

**Spacing:** Crowns or young plants 12–18in (30–45cm) apart, rows 18in (45cm) apart, staggered between rows. A pot at least 1ft (30cm) wide and deep will take one crown in the centre, but pot-grown plants have a shorter life and lighter crops than those in the open ground.

**Care:** A warm, sunny, sheltered, free-draining permanent bed – plants can crop for about 20 years – of several square yards, with no other crops grown among them and no replanting of an old asparagus bed in the same spot. Prepare thoroughly, or plant into a no-dig mulch. Water new plants through the first summer; once established they are drought-tolerant and need water only in long dry spells. Mulch thickly each late winter. Established plants on reasonably fertile, mulched ground rarely need feed; if growth is poor, a general fertiliser in early spring, before spears appear, can help. Weed carefully so shallow roots are not damaged, and remove female (berried) plants as advised in the care section. Stake and string a 'fence' either side of the row, optionally with netting about 20in (50cm) above the ground, so summer fern is not blown over and the crown damaged. After the fern has grown through summer, cut plants to ground level in autumn. Crowns can be divided in late winter or early spring, taking strong outer pieces with several growing points. Potted plants need regular watering, support against toppling, a balanced liquid feed in the growing season, and a spring top-dressing.

**Harvest window:** April–June, but take no spears for the first two years after planting (three if grown from seed). Cut individual spears with a sharp knife 1in (2.5cm) below the soil when they are no more than 7in (18cm) tall or about finger-thick. In warm weather, cut every two or three days. An established plant may give about 10 spears over the harvest period.

**Storing:** Eat spears promptly for flavour; no long store is described.

**Common problems:** Frost damage, asparagus beetle, slugs and snails.

**Varieties:**

- **'Backlim' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Connover's Colossal' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Gijnlim' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Guelph Millennium' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Pacific 2000'** (recommended)
- **Green asparagus** — Green asparagus is sold as straight, tapered spears, typically grown to about 9 inches (22 cm), though thickness varies. The flavour is mild and grassy; snap or trim off the fibrous ends, then eat the spears raw or cooked. The plant is native around the Mediterranean — southern Europe, north Africa and the Middle East — and has been recorded since about 3000 BCE.
- **Purple asparagus** — Purple asparagus is usually cut at 6–8 inches (15–20 cm), with slender to thick violet spears and a sweet, grassy, slightly nutty flavour. Thinner stems are the ones for raw salads; thicker ones are better cooked. Modern purple types are thought to be Italian, though asparagus as a crop goes back to Mesopotamia.

### Globe artichokes

**Sow:** Indoors or out (February–May). Under cover, about ¾in (2cm) deep in peat-free seed mix, kept warm, bright and watered. Outdoors, two or three seeds at the final spacing, later thinned to the strongest; fast catch-crops can fill the gaps between young plants.

**Plant out:** Late spring or early summer after the last frost (May–June). Indoor seedlings are ready at about 1ft (30cm) tall with at least five large leaves; harden off first. Bought plugs should be potted on until they reach a similar size and a strong root system. Plant at the same depth as before, firm and water. Water regularly in dry spells through the first summer.

**Spacing:** 30–35in (75–90cm) apart; plants can eventually spread to 3ft (1m) wide. A pot must be at least 18in (45cm) wide and deep, filled with peat-free compost, and will need frequent watering in dry weather. The crop is happier in the open ground.

**Care:** Warm, sunny, reasonably fertile, free-draining, sheltered ground – steer clear of chilly frost pockets and of ground that lies wet. Dig in two buckets of organic matter per square yard (square metre), or use a no-dig mulch. Little work is needed once plants are established. Water new plants through their first season, and in long hot droughts while buds are forming, or you may get fewer, smaller heads. Mulch in late winter or spring to hold moisture, and again in autumn; in very cold, frosty weather add cloches or biodegradable fleece. In late autumn, lay spent foliage over the crown, and in colder districts add a thick insulating layer of straw, bracken or garden compost. Feed is rarely needed; if cropping is poor, or in pots, a high-potassium organic fertiliser in spring can help bud production. In the first summer, remove flower stalks so the plant can establish; you can leave them to crop in the second summer. Divide mature clumps about every five years, or take spring offsets: vigorous outer rosettes 8–12in (20–30cm) tall, leaves cut back to 6in (15cm), planted at once.

**Harvest window:** June–September, once plants are in their second summer. Take the flower buds while they are still tightly closed. A mature plant may produce up to 12 buds in a season.

**Storing:** Closed buds are harvested for fresh use; no long store is given.

**Common problems:** Frost damage, aphids, slugs and snails.

**In the kitchen:** The globe artichoke is an unopened flower bud, typically 3–6 inches (7–15 cm) across, of tight, triangular, slightly spiny green bracts around a fleshy base. The flavour is nutty, tangy and grassy; very young buds can be eaten raw, while larger ones are cooked. It is a Mediterranean plant, thought to have been selected from the wild cardoon, a thistle prized in ancient Greece and Rome.

**Varieties:**

- **'Green Globe Improved'** (recommended)
- **'Purple Globe'** (recommended)
- **'Gros Vert de Laon'** (recommended)
- **Violet artichoke (carciofi violetti)** — Carciofi violetti are purple-bracted artichokes, round, cylindrical or elongated according to type, with fleshy, pliable scales and a mild, sweet, slightly bitter taste. Young inner leaves can be pulled and eaten; the heads are also cooked in the usual ways. They descend from wild Mediterranean thistles, with Sicily often cited as an early centre of selection.
- **Cardoon** — Cardoons are tall, pale grey-green, ribbed leaf stalks, typically 2–5 feet (60–150 cm) long, edged with tough fibres and sharp spines that must be stripped off (gloves are wise) before cooking. Once cleaned, the stalks are a traditional Mediterranean vegetable. The plant is native to north-west Africa and has grown wild for thousands of years, later spreading around the central and western Mediterranean.

### Jerusalem artichokes

**Sow:** Grown from tubers, not seed. Choose slightly smaller than hen's-egg size, not too knobbly. Plant February–May.

**Plant out:** Plant tubers 4–6in (10–15cm) deep in the final position. Water in well. They can reach 10ft (3m), so site them where they will not shade other crops; several rows can also serve as a windbreak. Compact types suit pots.

**Spacing:** 1ft (30cm) apart, in rows or staggered. Leave at least 3ft (1m) between rows if you need access, or close the rows to 1ft (30cm) to make a block. A pot at least 18in (45cm) wide and deep will take one or two tubers at the same depth; expect a smaller crop, and water regularly, especially in heat.

**Care:** Sunny site, moderately fertile, moisture-holding soil that does not lie waterlogged (tubers may rot), or plant into a no-dig mulch. Little maintenance is needed, but water in dry summer spells so tubers swell – drought can make them smaller and more knobbly – and stake plants unless the site is sheltered or the variety is compact. Mulch after planting in late winter or spring. When stems are about 1ft (30cm) tall, draw up a 6in (15cm) mound of soil around the base. On windy ground, place tall canes or woody stakes around the planting and loop string around the growth, or cut stems back to about 5ft (1.5m) in late July or August (also removing flowers). Cutting off flowers may slightly favour tuber size but is not essential. When leaves yellow in autumn, cut stems to about 3in (8cm) and lay the prunings, or cardboard or a thick mulch, over the bed in colder districts so the soil is easier to dig in hard frost.

**Harvest window:** November–February, from late autumn onward. Each plant should generally give 10 or more tubers. Frost is said to improve flavour, and they do not turn woody. Any scrap of tuber left behind will regrow, so clear them thoroughly if you do not want a return crop; in light soil they can run deep and wide.

**Storing:** They keep poorly once lifted, so leave them in the ground and dig as needed through winter. In cold districts, insulate the bed as above so you can still lift in hard frost. You can also leave a few tubers in place if you want the clump to continue.

**Common problems:** Sclerotinia disease, slugs and snails.

**In the kitchen:** Jerusalem artichokes, or sunchokes, are knobbly tubers; cultivated ones are typically 3–4 inches (7.5–10 cm) long, oblong or bulbous, while wild ones are thinner and more irregular, with thin, ringed skin. The flavour is mild, sweet and nutty, and they can be eaten raw or cooked, peeled or unpeeled. They are a North American sunflower, thought to have originated east of the Rocky Mountains, between what is now the United States and Canada.

**Varieties:**

- **'Dwarf Sunray'** (recommended)
- **'Fuseau'** (recommended)

### Rhubarb

**Sow:** Seed is the cheapest way to raise many plants but is neither the easiest nor the quickest. Sow under cover in late winter, 1in (2.5cm) deep in peat-free modules, or outdoors in spring in a nursery bed at the same depth, thinning to 6in (15cm) before moving young plants to their final spacing. Most gardeners start from crowns or potted plants instead.

**Plant out:** Potted plants can go in at any time, though spring or autumn is best, and not during a hot, dry spell. Bare-root crowns are usually offered from November to March and should go in soon after they arrive (calendar November–March). Sit the growing tip, or the point from which the leaf stalks emerge, just above soil level, firm and water. On winter-wet soil, or where space is tight, use a pot at least 20in (50cm) deep and wide, ideally with a compact variety, with the main bud just above the compost, and be ready to water through spring and summer.

**Spacing:** 30–35in (75–90cm) apart, as clumps soon become large.

**Care:** Open, sunny, fertile, moisture-holding ground that does not lie waterlogged, and not a frost pocket that could damage young stems. Use a no-dig mulch if that is your method. Once established, plants rarely need water, but new plants should be watered in dry spells through the first season, and pots must be kept moist but never waterlogged. Growth slows or stops in hot drought. Each spring, spread about 2½in (7cm) of well-rotted garden compost around, but not over, the crown. Mulch usually supplies enough nutrients; on poor soil, if growth is slow, a high-nitrogen organic fertiliser in spring or summer can help, though too much nitrogen, or a wet summer, makes flowering more likely – remove flower stems. Divide overcrowded or weakening clumps about every five years. Forcing: pot a crown into peat-free compost and bring it into a cool room or glasshouse at 45–60°F (7–16°C); forced crowns are much weakened and are usually discarded afterwards. Forced stalks are taken at about 1ft (30cm), often four to eight weeks after covering; then uncover the crown and leave that plant unpicked until the next year.

**Harvest window:** March–July. Take no stalks in the first year after planting. Choose a young stalk about 1ft (30cm) long whose leaf has only just fully opened, and never take more than about a third of the stems, so enough leaves remain to feed the plant.

**Storing:** Best used fresh, but it can be frozen or kept in a fridge for a couple of weeks.

**Common problems:** Plant viruses, slugs and snails.

**In the kitchen:** Rhubarb stalks are typically 4–16 inches (10–40 cm) long and ¾–2 inches (2–5 cm) thick, slender, flattened on one side and rounded on the other, in shades of red, pink and green according to variety, firm and tart, tangy and vegetal in flavour. Only the stalks are eaten; the leaf blades are not for the table. The plant is thought to have originated in Asia, possibly Siberia or China, and parts of it have a long history in traditional Chinese medicine.

**Varieties:**

- **'Victoria'** (recommended)
- **'Fulton's Strawberry Surprise' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Raspberry Red' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Timperley Early' AGM** (recommended)

## Fruit

### Apples

**Sow:** Named trees are bought on a chosen rootstock rather than raised from seed. For containers, use a dwarfing stock such as M9 or M26.

**Plant out:** While the tree is dormant, November–March. Pot-grown trees can go in outside that window, but not in heat and drought, and they may settle more slowly.

**Spacing:** Trained forms (cordon, espalier or fan) about 1ft (30cm) out from a wall or fence. In grass, clear a circle at least 3ft (1m) across so roots are not competing with turf. A pot should be at least 18in (45cm) wide, filled with a loam-based peat-free mix such as John Innes No. 3. Leave room for the tree's eventual size, which depends on rootstock and form. Step-overs stand only 18–24in (45–60cm) high.

**Care:** A fertile, free-draining, warm and sheltered site in full sun, away from late frosts that can ruin blossom. Water new trees through dry weather for their first two seasons; pot trees need even moisture in the growing months, but established trees in the ground rarely do. Mulch each spring with garden compost or well-rotted manure, keeping it off the trunk. Feed if growth or cropping is poor. Thin heavy sets, prune each winter (or as recommended for the trained form), and check stored fruit later in the year.

**Harvest window:** August–November, according to variety. Pick when fruits have sized up, begun to colour and taste sweet, or when windfalls appear. Cup the fruit, lift and twist lightly so it parts with the stalk attached. Eat windfalls promptly, as they may be bruised.

**Storing:** Early (late-summer) kinds are usually eaten within a few days. Later keepers can last several months if unblemished: set them in a single layer in slatted trays so they do not touch, in a cool, dark, frost-free, airy place, and inspect often. Cooked culinary apples freeze well; dessert kinds can be frozen too, though they soften on thawing.

**Common problems:** Bitter pit, apple canker, apple scab, biennial bearing, brown rot, codling moth, honey fungus, fruit aphids, powdery mildews.

- **Newton Wonder** — Newton Wonder is a large cooker, typically 4–4½ inches (10–11 cm) across, bulbous and slightly conical, green-yellow heavily striped and flushed dark red-orange, with sour, crisp, dense flesh that cooks down to a creamy, light purée with a tart-sweet taste. - **Annie Elizabeth** — Annie Elizabeth is a medium to large, irregular, slightly conical apple, typically 3–3½ inches (8–9 cm) across and 2½–2¾ inches (6–7 cm) tall, often ribbed and ‘hammered’ in appearance. It is sharp raw and becomes sweet and tangy when cooked, and is known for holding its shape in the pan. It is thought to have come from a chance Blenheim Orange seedling, developed by Samuel Greatorex in Leicestershire from 1855 and named in 1866; it became a favoured Victorian cooking apple.
- **Adams Pearmain** — Adams Pearmain is a small to medium, conical apple, typically 2–2½ inches (5–6 cm) across, pale yellow-green flushed dark red, spotted with grey lenticels and patched with russet on the shade side. The flavour is aromatic and sweet-tart, used fresh or cooked, with or without the skin. Parentage and date are unknown; it may once have been called Hanging Pearmain and come from Herefordshire, as Robert Hogg noted, and was recorded nationally in 1826 when Sir Robert Adams gave scion wood to the London Horticultural Society.
- **Ellison’s Orange** — Ellison’s Orange is a small to medium, oval to conical apple, typically 2–2½ inches (5–6 cm) across, yellow-green flushed brick-red, with a rich, sweet-tart flavour usually eaten fresh. - **Tydeman’s Early** — Tydeman’s Early is a small to medium, often slightly lopsided apple, typically 2–2½ inches (5–6 cm) across, with thin, smooth, slightly greasy skin and a mild, sweet flavour used as a dessert fruit. Researchers at East Malling, in Maidstone, Kent, raised it in 1929.
- **Bloody Ploughman** — Bloody Ploughman is a medium to large, ribbed, slightly flattened apple, typically 2½–3 inches (6–8 cm) across, golden-yellow almost covered in a deep crimson-purple flush, with a sticky wax and, in some ripe fruits, pink-stained flesh. The flavour is delicate and sweet, and the apple is used both as a dessert and in the kitchen. It is a Scottish heirloom from the Carse of Gowrie; parentage is unknown, and it was first recorded in 1883.
- **Orléans Reinette** — Orléans Reinette is a medium to large, squat, slightly flattened apple, typically 2¾–3 inches (7–8 cm) across, with taut, matte skin marked by prominent lenticels and light ribbing. The flavour is sweet, tart and citrusy, used fresh or cooked, and it was traditionally a dessert fruit. It is thought to be French, first recorded around 1776, though pomologists suspect it may be older.

### Apricots

**Sow:** Bought as named trees, not usually raised from seed.

**Plant out:** While dormant, November–March. A warm, sheltered wall facing south, south-west or west is best, so the tree can be trained as a fan. In milder, very sheltered spots a free-standing tree on a stout stake may crop; in cold districts an unheated glasshouse or polytunnel is safer.

**Spacing:** Plant about 1ft (30cm) out from the wall. Fan-trained trees need a wide stretch of wall, typically 11–16ft (3.5–5m). A pot should be at least 18in (45cm) wide. Horizontal training wires are put up before planting.

**Care:** Deep, fertile soil that holds moisture but drains. Water new trees in dry spells for the first two years; fans at the foot of a wall, pot trees, and glasshouse trees need extra moisture, especially while fruit is swelling in early to mid-summer. Established free-standing trees rarely need watering. Mulch each spring, leaving a gap at the stem. Protect early blossom from frost, hand-pollinate if insects are scarce, and thin heavy crops. Bring pot trees under cover for winter if needed. Prune annually.

**Harvest window:** July–August. Fruit is ready from late July, when it is soft, richly coloured and parts easily. Handle gently, as it bruises readily.

**Storing:** Best eaten at once; it keeps only a few days. Fruit can also be dried or made into preserves.

**Common problems:** Bacterial canker, blossom wilt, honey fungus, fruit aphids, glasshouse red spider mite, grey squirrels, silver leaf, spotted wing drosophila, tortrix moth caterpillars.

**Varieties:**

- **'Flavorcot' (syn. 'Bayoto')** (recommended)
- **'Moorpark'** (recommended) — Moorpark is a large apricot, typically 2–2¾ inches (5–7 cm) across, like a small peach, golden-orange and, when fully ripe, sometimes brownish-red or spotted on the sunny side, with thin aromatic skin, deep yellow to orange freestone flesh, and a richly sweet, plum-like flavour. It can be eaten raw, or dried, puréed, roasted and grilled, and pairs well with other stone fruit. It was introduced in England in 1760 (some think earlier, even in the 16th century), taking its name from the Hertfordshire estate of Moor Park, and first sold under that name in 1788.
- **'Tomcot'** (recommended)
- **Blenheim apricot** — Blenheim is a small apricot, typically ¾–2 inches (2–5 cm) across, round to oval with a clear seam, lightly fuzzy golden-orange skin sometimes blushed red, easily bruised, and often with a slight green tinge on the shoulders. The flavour is sweet-tart, used fresh, cooked or dried; the fruit ripens from the inside out. Its genealogy is disputed: one story links it to the Luxembourg Gardens in Paris, introduced around 1815 as ‘Royal’; another says a seed from that tree was later planted in the garden of Blenheim.

### Blackberries and hybrid berries

**Sow:** Usually planted as canes, not raised from seed.

**Plant out:** Mid-autumn to early spring is ideal (calendar October–March, also January–February). Pot-grown plants can go in at other times, but not in heat and drought. Set container plants at the same depth as before; with bare-root canes, the top roots should sit about 2in (5cm) below the surface.

**Spacing:** At least 1ft (30cm) out from a wall. Moderately vigorous kinds 8–11ft (2.5–3.5m) apart; very vigorous kinds up to 13ft (4m). Compact types can go in a pot at least 18in (45cm) wide and deep, filled with a loam-based peat-free mix such as John Innes No. 3. After planting, cut stems back to sound buds 8–10in (20–25cm) above the ground.

**Care:** Sun or a reasonably open site, on ground improved with organic matter if it is very chalky, sandy or poorly drained. Water well at planting and through dry weather in the first season; established plants in the ground rarely need extra water, but pots should stay slightly moist. In early spring lay a 2–3in (5–7.5cm) organic mulch, keeping it off the stems. Regularly mulched ground seldom needs extra feed. Train along horizontal wires and prune as recommended. Net, or use a fruit cage, to keep birds off ripening fruit.

**Harvest window:** July–September, from mid-summer into early autumn according to type. Pick as soon as berries are plump and juicy; they will not sweeten further once off the plant. Gather every few days into a shallow punnet, only two or three layers deep, so they are not crushed.

**Storing:** Eat fresh, freeze, or use in jams, jellies and puddings.

**Common problems:** Grey mould, raspberry viruses, raspberry and blackberry aphids, raspberry beetle, raspberry leaf and bud mite, red berry mite.

**In the kitchen:** Blackberries are dark aggregate fruits of sprawling bushes, typically 1–1½ inches (2.5–4 cm) long and ⅜–1 inch (1–2.5 cm) wide, made of tiny drupelets with almost invisible hairs. Eat them fresh, or bake, ferment and preserve them as jam, jelly and compote. They have grown wild for centuries in Asia, Europe and the Americas, preferring a mild, humid climate.

**Varieties:**

- **'Tayberry' AGM** (recommended)
- **Loganberry 'Ly 654' AGM** (recommended) — Loganberries grow on vigorous brambles as bright-red aggregate fruits of firm, plump drupelets, medium to large, up to about 1½ inches (4 cm) across, elongated like a raspberry but solid at the core. Use them as you would raspberries or blackberries; the ripest are best eaten fresh, while younger, tarter berries suit cooking. They were first raised in the late 1800s by James H. Logan, an attorney and horticulturist in Santa Cruz, California.
- **Blackberry 'Loch Ness' AGM** (recommended)
- **Blackberry 'Loch Tay' AGM** (recommended)

### Blackcurrants

**Sow:** Usually planted as bushes; hardwood cuttings are a common way to increase stock. Seed is not the usual method.

**Plant out:** In the dormant season, late October to March (calendar November–March, also January–February), avoiding frozen or waterlogged ground. Pot-grown bushes can go in at other times if you water them well in dry spells.

**Spacing:** Set both pot-grown and bare-root bushes about 2in (5cm) deeper than they stood before, to encourage strong basal shoots (standards stay at the previous depth and need a stout stake). Space bushes 5–6ft (1.5–1.8m) apart. A pot should be at least 18in (45cm) wide.

**Care:** Water new plants in dry weather for the first two years; established bushes in the ground rarely need routine watering. Pots should stay evenly moist, but ease off when fruit is colouring, or skins may split. Mulch each spring. Protect blossom from frost and ripening fruit from birds. Prune annually.

**Harvest window:** July–August, from midsummer according to variety. On many newer cultivars (the Ben series is typical), a whole strig (bunch) colours together and can be snipped off; older kinds ripen from the top of the bunch first, so pick berries one by one. Handle gently. A mature bush may yield about 10lb (4.5kg).

**Storing:** Eat within a few days, refrigerating if needed. Fruit freezes successfully, either uncooked or after cooking, and also makes good preserves.

**Common problems:** Blackcurrant big bud mite, blackcurrant gall midge, currant and gooseberry leaf spot, gooseberry mildew, currant aphids, pigeons.

**In the kitchen:** Blackcurrants are tiny berries, usually less than ⅜ inch (1 cm) across, round to oval, with a dried brown calyx at the tip, and a savoury, astringent, slightly sweet flavour used fresh or, more often, cooked. They are native to central and eastern Europe and parts of northern Asia, and have grown wild in temperate forests for a very long time.

**Varieties:**

- **'Ben Sarek'** (recommended)
- **'Ben Connan' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Big Ben' AGM** (recommended)
- **Jostaberry** — Jostaberries are typically ⅜–1¼ inches (1–3 cm) across, round, flattened or oval, hanging in clusters of three to five, green when young and darkening as they ripen, with a fruity, sweet-sour flavour used raw or cooked. They are a European creation, developed through crosses in the 19th and 20th centuries to combine gooseberry and blackcurrant traits.

### Blueberries

**Sow:** Planted as bushes, not usually raised from seed.

**Plant out:** They settle best while dormant, mid-autumn to early spring (calendar October–March). They can go in at other times except in heat and drought or into frozen ground. Choose a sheltered position in sun or light shade, on moisture-retentive, acidic soil that neither dries in summer nor sits wet in winter. In a pot, use peat-free ericaceous compost.

**Spacing:** In the ground, 3¼–6½ft (1–2m) apart according to eventual size, with the soil only just covering the roots. A pot should be at least 12in (30cm) across for a young plant, and 18–20in (45–50cm) for a larger one.

**Care:** Water in at planting and through dry spells in the first year; later, extra water is mainly needed in long droughts while fruit is forming, and regularly for pots and raised beds. Prefer rainwater, as tap water is often alkaline and will raise pH if used often. Mulch with an ericaceous material. Feed only if cropping is poor or leaves show a nutrient shortage, using an ericaceous fertiliser. Protect pots in winter if needed, and net the crop against birds.

**Harvest window:** July–September, from mid-summer. Berries turn from green to dusky blue and do not all ripen together, so pick over the bush for several weeks, easing berries off individually. A plant around seven years old may give 5–11lb (2.25–5kg).

**Storing:** Usually eaten fresh; a long store method is not spelled out.

**Common problems:** Aphids, nutrient deficiencies, Phytophthora root rot, powdery mildews, spotted wing drosophila, vine weevil.

**In the kitchen:** Blueberries are small, typically ¼–⅝ inch (0.5–1.6 cm) across, round to slightly flattened, with a five-pointed, star-shaped calyx at the stem end. The flavour is sweet and faintly tangy, used raw or cooked. They are native to North America, where several wild species have been gathered since ancient times, including by indigenous peoples.

**Varieties:**

- **'Ozarkblue'** (recommended)
- **'Spartan' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Duke' AGM** (recommended)

### Cherries

**Sow:** Bought as named trees on a chosen rootstock, not usually raised from seed. Many modern sweet cherries are self-fertile, so one tree can be enough.

**Plant out:** While dormant, November–March. A warm, sheltered site away from late frosts. Sweet cherries want full sun (a south or south-west wall suits a fan); acid cherries tolerate some shade and can go on a north-facing wall. They do best in southern and central England, on deep, fertile, free-draining, slightly acidic soil (ideally pH 6.5–6.7), not shallow, sandy or waterlogged ground.

**Spacing:** In grass, clear at least 3¼ft (1m) around the trunk. A fan should stand 10–14in (25–35cm) out from the wall, with wires already in place. A pot should be 18–20in (45–50cm) wide.

**Care:** Water new trees whenever rain is scarce through year one at least, and keep pot trees evenly moist in the growing season without leaving them standing in water. Fans at the foot of a wall may need extra water. Established trees in open ground rarely do. Mulch in spring, protect blossom and ripening fruit (especially from birds), and prune as recommended for the form.

**Harvest window:** June–August, from early summer according to variety. Pick only fully ripe fruit, in dry weather if you can, holding the stalk rather than the cherry, which bruises easily. Fruit will not ripen further once picked.

**Storing:** Best eaten fresh; a long store method is not spelled out.

**Common problems:** Cherry fruit drop, bacterial canker, blossom wilt, brown rot, cherry leaf scorch and leaf spot, honey fungus, fruit aphids, silver leaf, spotted wing drosophila.

**Varieties:**

- **'Stella' AGM** (recommended) — Stella is a medium to large sweet cherry, typically 1–1½ inches (2.5–3.5 cm) across, round to heart-shaped, on stems about 1½–1¾ inches (4–4.5 cm) long. The flavour is sweet and tangy, used fresh or cooked, and the fruit is often eaten straight from the tree. It was bred in the mid-20th century by Dr Charles Lapins at the Summerland research centre in British Columbia.
- **'Kordia' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Lapins' AGM** (recommended) — Lapins is a large cherry, typically ¾–1½ inches (2–4 cm) across, heart-shaped to slightly conical, with thin, glossy ruby to crimson skin and a mild, sweet flavour used fresh or cooked. Dr Charles Lapins of the Summerland centre in British Columbia raised it in the early 1970s, from a Stella × Van cross.
- **'Morello' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Summer Sun' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Sweetheart' AGM** (recommended) — Sweetheart is a small to medium, evenly heart-shaped cherry with thin, glossy skin from bright red to almost black, and a sweet, tangy flavour mainly enjoyed fresh. It was also raised at the Summerland Research and Development Centre in the Okanagan Valley of British Columbia.
- **Sunburst** — Sunburst is a medium to large cherry, typically about 1¼–1½ inches (3–3.5 cm) across, oval to heart-shaped, on stems about 1½ inches (3.5–4 cm) long, with a mild, sweet flavour and generous flesh, used fresh or cooked. Sour cherries are thought to be native to eastern Europe and south-western Asia, including the Caucasus between the Black Sea and the Caspian.

### Citrus

**Sow:** Usually bought as pot plants. Seed-raising is not the usual method.

**Plant out:** Grown year-round in containers that can stand outdoors in a warm, sheltered spot in summer and come under cover for winter. They need bright light and protection from UK cold.

**Spacing:** Grown in pots rather than at a stated in-ground spacing. As a rough guide, a tree about 3ft (1m) tall should carry no more than about 20 fruits at once (kumquats, being smaller, can hold more).

**Care:** Year-round attention: in summer keep compost just moist, checking daily in heat, preferably with rainwater; in winter let the surface partly dry, then water thoroughly with tepid rainwater and never leave the pot standing in water – winter overwatering is a common failure. Raise humidity if the plant is in a heated room. Feed with a citrus fertiliser. Repot when needed. Hand-pollinate flowers if insects are scarce, and thin heavy sets. Winter minimums vary: some lemons tolerate down to 42°F (5°C), kumquats often want 45°F (7°C) or more, limes and grapefruits above 50°F (10°C), and calamondin oranges at least 55°F (13°C).

**Harvest window:** Year-round, as fruit often takes nine to twelve months to mature. Once it has reached full size and a rich skin colour, cut it off with a short piece of stem, using secateurs rather than pulling, which can tear the wood.

**Storing:** Sound fruit will keep for several weeks in cool, airy conditions.

**Common problems:** Citrus problems (general container and indoor issues), aphids, glasshouse red spider mite, mealybug, scale insects, tortrix moth caterpillars.

**Varieties:**

- **Citrus japonica AGM** (recommended)
- **Citrus × aurantiifolia** (recommended)
- **Citrus x limon 'Garey's Eureka'** (recommended)
- **Citrus × aurantium Grapefruit Group** (recommended)
- **Citrus x microcarpa AGM** (recommended)

### Damsons

**Sow:** Bought as named trees, not usually raised from seed.

**Plant out:** While dormant, usually November–March. A warm, sheltered, sunny site; blossom is easily damaged by frost and wind, so in colder spots train the tree as a fan against a wall or fence that faces south, south-west or west. Fertile, slightly acidic soil (ideally pH 6–6.5) that does not dry out while fruit forms – loamy or other moisture-holding soils are fine if they are not waterlogged.

**Spacing:** In grass, clear at least 3¼ft (1m) around the trunk. A fan should stand 10–14in (25–35cm) out from the wall. A pot should be at least 20in (50cm) wide. Bush trees are often formed with a trunk about 2½ft (75cm) tall; pyramids have branches starting 16–20in (40–50cm) above the ground.

**Care:** Water new trees through dry weather in at least the first season; established trees in suitable soil rarely need extra water, though wall-trained trees in a rain shadow may. Keep pot compost evenly moist from April to August, without leaving pots standing in water. Mulch in spring (a 2–3in / 5–8cm layer is typical), protect blossom from frost, thin heavy crops so branches do not break, and prune in summer as for plums.

**Harvest window:** July–September. Flavour is best if fruit is left to finish on the tree; it is ready when it yields to a gentle squeeze. Trees usually need several pickings. Handle gently, as fruit bruises easily.

**Storing:** Use promptly for desserts, jam and other preserves, or to flavour gin. A long fresh store is not described.

**Common problems:** Bacterial canker, blossom wilt, biennial bearing, brown rot, honey fungus, pocket plum, silver leaf, plum aphids, plum moth.

**In the kitchen:** Damsons are small stone fruit, about the size of a large cherry, round to slightly pointed, with taut, dark blue to red-purple skin under a powdery bloom, and golden to yellow-green, fairly dry, dense flesh. They can be eaten fresh, but the sweet-tart, bitter flavour is often too astringent raw, and they are famous for cooking, especially jams and preserves. The type is native to the Middle East, particularly around Damascus in Syria — the name is thought to come from the city — and stones have been found in Mesopotamian ruins.

**Varieties:**

### Figs

**Sow:** Bought as plants, not usually raised from seed.

**Plant out:** Spring (March–April) is the preferred time, so the plant has a full season to settle. A warm, sunny, sheltered site, ideally a south or south-west wall or fence for fan training. Restricting the roots encourages fruiting.

**Spacing:** Allow a fan about 11½ft (3.5m) of width, more if you have room, and plant about 1ft (30cm) out from the wall. To confine roots, dig a pit 2ft (60cm) deep and wide, line the sides with paving slabs standing 1in (2.5cm) above soil level, leave the base open, and add a 4–8in (10–20cm) layer of rubble for drainage. A large pot (or a pot sunk in the ground) is an alternative.

**Care:** Because roots are usually confined, water through the growing season so the soil stays evenly moist – check daily in hot, dry weather. Wall-side plants may sit in a rain shadow. Too little water can cause leaf and fruit drop; waterlogging is also harmful. Mulch, feed regularly, and protect the plant in winter. Net ripening fruit against squirrels and birds, holding the mesh taut and clear of the figs. Prune and train as a fan if grown on a wall.

**Harvest window:** July–September. In most sites only one crop ripens, at the end of summer; in a very warm spot or a glasshouse there may be two. Fruit is ready when the skin is soft, the fig hangs limply, and it may split under a gentle squeeze or show a drop of sugary liquid at the eye. Hold the stalk and tear it from the branch.

**Storing:** Best eaten soon after picking; fruit can also be frozen or made into preserves.

**Common problems:** Honey fungus, fig leaf skeletoniser, glasshouse red spider mite, grey squirrels, mealybug, scale insects, spotted wing drosophila.

**Varieties:**

- **LITTLE MISS FIGGY 'Lmf01' PBR** (recommended)
- **'White Marseilles'** (recommended)
- **'Brown Turkey' AGM** (recommended) — Brown Turkey is a medium to large fig, typically ¾–2¾ inches (2–7 cm) across and 2–4 inches (5–10 cm) long, oblate, pear-shaped or slightly lopsided, sometimes with a thick, curved neck. The flavour is sweet, mild and slightly nutty, used fresh or cooked. The fig itself comes from western Asia and the eastern Mediterranean, and is thought to be among the oldest cultivated fruits.

### Goji berries

**Sow:** Seed can be sown indoors in spring, barely covered with a very fine layer of compost, and germinated at 64–68°F (18–20°C). Increase plants by summer cuttings (soft or half-ripe wood), winter hardwood cuttings, autumn layers, or suckers lifted in late winter. Give young plants winter protection in their first year and move them to their final spot the following spring.

**Plant out:** Late summer to autumn, in free-draining ground improved with well-rotted garden compost or manure. Full sun gives the best crop, though light shade is tolerated. Once established, plants are hardy and will stand wind, coastal salt and drought.

**Spacing:** At least 6½ft (2m) apart. A mature shrub may reach about 10ft (3m) tall and 13ft (4m) or more wide. For a pot, choose a container with good drainage and a John Innes No. 2 mix with added grit or sharp sand.

**Care:** In spring apply a general fertiliser, such as Growmore, at 2oz per sq yd (70g per sq m). Pot plants can have a liquid feed, such as a tomato fertiliser, every fortnight in the growing season. For the heaviest crop, train the lax, spiny stems on wires against a wall or fence, wearing gloves. Flowers and fruit form on the previous year's wood: in early spring take out dead and badly placed shoots, shorten overlong stems if needed, and remove some of the oldest wood. Container plants can have new growth cut back by up to half in summer, though this reduces yield. Control suckers.

**Harvest window:** From late summer until the first frosts, once plants are two to three years old. Only fully ripe berries are edible. Fruit can blacken when handled, so it can help to shake ripe berries onto a sheet placed beneath the plant.

**Storing:** Berries can be eaten fresh, cooked or dried.

**Common problems:** Aphids; birds (netting may be needed); suckers can become invasive.

### Gooseberries

**Sow:** Usually planted as bushes or trained plants; cuttings are a common way to increase stock.

**Plant out:** Bare-root plants from late autumn to early spring; pot-grown plants at other times except in heat and drought (calendar November–March). Most soils are tolerated if they are moist and free-draining. A sunny, sheltered site gives the sweetest fruit, but light shade – including a north-facing wall – is acceptable. Avoid windy spots (stems are brittle) and late-frost hollows that can spoil flowers. A fruit cage helps against birds.

**Spacing:** Bushes and standards 4–5ft (1.2–1.5m) apart; cordons 12–15in (30–38cm); fans 3¼–5ft (1–1.5m). A pot should be at least 16in (40cm) wide and deep, with ample drainage, filled with a soil-based compost. Horizontal wires for fans and cordons are usually set at 2ft (60cm) and 4ft (1.2m). Cane supports up to 5½ft (1.7m) for cordons and 3¼ft (1m) for standards.

**Care:** Water new plants in dry spells for the first two years; established plants in the ground rarely need extra water, but pots should stay evenly moist. Mulch each spring and prune to keep plants open and productive. Protect buds and fruit from birds.

**Harvest window:** June–August, in two passes: in June, while berries are still green and under-ripe, take every other fruit for jam, pies and sauces, leaving the rest to swell; in July and August pick the remainder when fully ripe and sweet, handling them carefully as they burst easily. A mature bush may give 6–12lb (2.5–5.5kg) a year, a cordon about 2lb (1kg).

**Storing:** Use green berries for cooking and ripe ones fresh or in preserves. A long fresh store is not spelled out.

**Common problems:** Currant and gooseberry leaf spot, brown scale, capsid bugs, gooseberry mildew, grey mould, fruit aphids, gooseberry sawfly, spotted wing drosophila, woolly vine or currant scale.

**Varieties:**

- **'Greenfinch' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Invicta' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Leveller' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Careless' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Whinham's Industry' AGM** (recommended)
- **Green gooseberry** — Green gooseberries vary widely by cultivar: smooth or fuzzy, from smaller than a blueberry to the size of a large cherry tomato, round or slightly oblong, with a sweet-tart flavour used in both sweet and savoury dishes, raw, cooked, mashed or preserved. Gooseberries are native to the Caucasus and North Africa, and even so-called American types trace back to early European stock.
- **Captivator gooseberry** — Captivator gooseberries are small, round to teardrop fruits, about 1 inch (2.5 cm) across, rather like red grapes, with semi-translucent skin that colours as it ripens, grown in clusters or singly on large bushes. ### Grapes

**Sow:** Planted as vines, not usually raised from seed.

**Plant out:** While dormant, November to early March (the calendar marks this as Plant Out). Early spring is a good outdoor time, so young plants avoid winter damage. Choose a warm, sheltered, sunny site, often the foot of a south-facing wall, with supports already in place, away from late frosts that can spoil new shoots. Soil pH 6.5–7 is preferred, but a range of free-draining soils will do.

**Spacing:** At least 8in (20cm) out from a wall or fence. In the open, vines 4–5ft (1.2–1.5m) apart, with rows 5–6ft (1.5–1.8m) apart running north–south if you plant a small vineyard. In a glasshouse, at least 3½ft (1m) apart if more than one vine is grown. A pot should be at least 12in (30cm) wide and deep, with a soil-based peat-free compost.

**Care:** Water new vines in dry spells in their first season. Established vines with roots outside rarely need extra water, except against a wall in a rain shadow; glasshouse and pot vines need regular watering in the growing season. Mulch outdoor vines in late winter or early spring with a 2–3in (5–7.5cm) layer of organic matter. Feed, thin flowers and bunches as advised, and follow the indoor or outdoor training method you have chosen.

**Harvest window:** August–October, from late summer to mid-autumn according to variety and weather. Dessert grapes are ready when soft and sugary; white kinds often turn from deep green to a thinner, translucent yellow. Taste is the surest test, as they will not sweeten further once cut. Cut the whole bunch with the stalk attached. Wine grapes can be eaten but are usually small, sharper and thicker-skinned, and are better crushed for wine. Young leaves can be used in cooking.

**Storing:** Use promptly once ripe; a long fresh store is not spelled out.

**Common problems:** Grape shanking, nutrient deficiencies, brown rot, brown scale, grapevine diseases (downy mildew, powdery mildew and others), grapevine blister mite, spotted wing drosophila, wasps, woolly vine or currant scale.

**Varieties:**

- **'Sovereign Coronation' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Reliance' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Schuyler' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Lakemont' AGM** (recommended)

### Honeyberry

**Sow:** Easily raised from seed in summer after the pulp has been washed off the seed, sown in moist warmth – no cold period is needed. Semi-ripe cuttings in summer from non-fruiting stems, or hardwood cuttings in late autumn or winter, also work and keep the parent plant's character.

**Plant out:** In spring, once frost risk has passed, so the shrub can settle through the summer. Full sun ripens the wood and improves the crop. Most soils will do, including acid or alkaline ground, so long as drainage is sound and organic matter is present – a useful alternative where blueberries are difficult. Plant at least two, or a group, to improve pollination; some suppliers sell them in pairs.

**Spacing:** Eventual size is given as about 4ft (1.2m); a precise in-ground spacing is not stated. Plant in pairs or groups.

**Care:** A yearly spring dressing of a balanced organic fertiliser (blood, fish and bone is the example given) is enough – too much feed makes leafy growth at the expense of flowers and fruit. A mulch of well-rotted organic matter, about 2in (5cm) thick, helps hold moisture. Flowers open in late winter or very early spring, when few insects are about, so hand-pollination with a soft brush can lift the crop. For the first three years, only take out dead wood; once established, prune in early to mid-summer after picking: remove weak or damaged growth, thin crowded shoots to the base, and pinch the tips of young shoots to encourage flowering laterals.

**Harvest window:** Late spring to early summer. Berries turn blue with a whitish bloom, but check that the flesh is purple-red – green, under-ripe fruit is very sour. A mature plant may give about 13lb (6kg). The first pickings may not come until the plant is around two years old.

**Storing:** Eat raw, or use in jams and jellies. A long fresh store is not described.

**Common problems:** Generally trouble-free; the plants are very hardy (down to -40°C, which is also -40°F). Hazard: many ornamental honeysuckles have inedible or harmful berries, so check you have an edible form (Lonicera caerulea).

**Varieties:**

- **Lonicera caerulea var. edulis** (recommended)
- **Lonicera caerulea var. kamtschatica** (recommended)

### Kiwi

**Sow:** Planted as named vines, not usually raised from seed. Self-fertile cultivars exist (for example 'Issai' and 'Jenny'); 'Hayward' is a well-known fruiting type that may need a pollinator depending on how it is sold.

**Plant out:** Spring (March–May), once soil and weather are warming. A warm, sheltered, sunny position, preferably a south- or west-facing wall; in milder districts they can stand in the open. Avoid frost pockets – young shoots are easily damaged in spring. Provide a stout support such as a pergola, arch, or strong wires on a wall or solid fence. Fertile, free-draining, slightly acidic soil with plenty of organic matter worked into the backfill. Prune immediately after planting.

**Spacing:** If planting more than one, 10–15ft (3–4.5m) apart. Other measurements in the training notes include laterals at about 16–20in (40–50cm) and 10–12in (25–30cm) spacings along wires, and planting about 1ft (30cm) out from a wall.

**Care:** Keep new vines watered through the growing months for several seasons after planting; later, extra water is mainly useful in long droughts while fruit is swelling. Roots rot in waterlogged ground, so drainage must be free. Mulch in early spring with garden compost or well-rotted manure, keeping it off the stem. Protect young growth from frost. Feed if needed, and follow the espalier or other training method from the start.

**Harvest window:** August–September. Vines often start cropping three or four years after planting, and need a long, warm summer and autumn to ripen fully outdoors, especially in southern England. Fruit that has not finished ripening can be picked in autumn, before a hard frost, and brought indoors to finish. Ripe fruit yields slightly to a squeeze.

**Storing:** Ripen in a single layer at room temperature, which may take several weeks; once ripe, fruit will keep chilled for about a fortnight.

**Common problems:** Nutrient deficiencies, honey fungus, Phytophthora root rot.

**Varieties:**

- **Actinidia arguta 'Issai'** (recommended)
- **Actinidia deliciosa 'Jenny'** (recommended)
- **Actinidia deliciosa 'Hayward'** (recommended)

### Medlars

**Sow:** Bought as named trees, not usually raised from seed.

**Plant out:** While dormant, late autumn to early spring (calendar November–March). Pot-grown trees can go in at other times, but not in heat and drought. Deep, fertile, free-draining soil is ideal; most types will do except very chalky or waterlogged ground. Full sun is preferred, though they will still crop in partial shade. Choose a warm, sheltered spot – leaves and flowers are easily spoiled by wind and late frost.

**Spacing:** Trees make a wide canopy, so keep them at least 16½ft (5m) from other trees or buildings. In grass, clear about 3¼ft (1m) around the trunk.

**Care:** Water new trees through dry weather in the first season, and young trees in long droughts for a couple of years after that; established trees in suitable soil rarely need extra water. Towards the end of winter or as spring begins, spread a 2–3in (5–7.5cm) mulch of garden compost, leaving a gap at the trunk. Feed only if cropping is poor. Prune as recommended for the form.

**Harvest window:** October–November. Pick after the fruit has had a frost, in dry weather, when the stalk parts easily. In the UK the fruit is still hard and bitter on the tree and is not eaten at that stage – it must be bletted (ripened in store) until it is soft, sweet and aromatic, even though it then looks brown.

**Storing:** To blet, lay fruit in a single layer on trays in a cool, dark, frost-free place until it softens. This is ripening, not rotting. Use once soft and sweet.

**Common problems:** Brown rot, powdery mildews, quince leaf blight.

**Varieties:**

- **'Iranian' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Nottingham'** (recommended) — Nottingham is a small medlar, typically 1–2 inches (3–5 cm) across, round to ovate, with tough, brown, almost russeted skin and a large open calyx at one end. The fruit must be bletted — left to soften — before it is ready, when the flesh becomes sticky and paste-like. Medlar is an ancient fruit from a region running from the south-eastern Balkans into Iran and north to the Crimea.

### Melons

**Sow:** These tender plants must be started under cover, ideally in a heated propagator, from mid-April to mid-May. Sow two seeds about ½in (1.5cm) deep in a 3in (7.5cm) pot, at 64–70°F (18–21°C); a consistently warm, sunny sill may also work. Seedlings usually appear in about a week – if both come, keep the stronger one. Grow them on in warmth, good light and regular moisture.

**Plant out:** Late May or June, once plants have three or four leaves and you can move them without a sharp temperature drop. Under cover, wait until temperatures stay reliably above 54–59°F (12–15°C). Outdoors they need a very warm, sheltered spot and are often given cloches or other covers.

**Spacing:** One plant to a container no smaller than 16in (40cm) in width and depth, filled with peat-free multi-purpose potting mix, or one plant to a standard growing bag. In a glasshouse border, 18in (45cm) apart; outdoors, about 3ft (90cm) apart.

**Care:** Train up strong, well-anchored supports or let plants trail. Water regularly, mulch, and feed. Keep them warm, and help with pollination under cover if insects are scarce. Pinch shoots and limit the number of fruits so those that remain can swell. Support heavy fruit with slings or netting. Have covers ready if outdoor nights turn cold.

**Harvest window:** July–October. Under glass, fruit may ripen from late July into early autumn; outdoors most fruit waits until late summer and the season is shorter once nights cool. Ripe melons have their typical scent and the stalk begins to crack; the blossom end yields slightly to pressure.

**Storing:** Eat when ripe; a long store is not described.

**Common problems:** Courgette, marrow, pumpkin and squash problems (cucurbit issues).

**Varieties:**

- **'Alvaro' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Emir' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Ogen' AGM** (recommended)

### Mulberries

**Sow:** Bought as trees, not usually raised from seed.

**Plant out:** Bare-root and pot-grown trees are best planted while dormant, autumn to spring (calendar October–April, also January–March); they often settle most readily in spring as the soil warms. Choose a sunny, sheltered site with deep, moisture-holding but free-draining soil, and room for the tree's wide, spreading crown. In colder districts a warm, sunny wall helps. Stake at planting.

**Spacing:** Leave plenty of space for the eventual spread – check the label. Compact kinds can go in a large pot (about 20in / 50cm is mentioned in the planting notes).

**Care:** Water new trees through dry spells for the first few years; later they rarely need routine watering except in long droughts or on very free-draining soil. Pot trees should stay evenly moist in the growing season. Mulch, feed if needed, and support heavy branches as the crown widens. Established trees in the ground are hardy and long-lived and need little else.

**Harvest window:** August–September. Black mulberries are slow to start, often around eight years, though smaller types may fruit in about half that time. Fruit looks like a large blackberry and is ready when it turns from red to glossy black, soft and juicy. Pick lower fruit by hand (gloves help if you want to avoid stained fingers); from a large tree, shake branches over a sheet.

**Storing:** Eat raw or make into jam. A long fresh store is not spelled out.

**Common problems:** Mulberry leaf spot, coral spot, powdery mildews.

**In the kitchen:** Mulberries are elongated, cylindrical fruits of densely packed drupelets around a central stem, typically 1–1½ inches (2.5–4 cm) long, looking rather like a stretched blackberry. They can be eaten fresh, or puréed, cooked, dried, preserved and juiced, including in ice cream and other puddings. They originated in China around 4,000 years ago, in the Western Zhou period, and like a warm, sunny climate.

**Varieties:**

- **Morus nigra** (recommended)
- **'Chelsea' AGM** (recommended)

### Nectarines

**Sow:** Bought as named trees, not usually raised from seed.

**Plant out:** Late autumn is often a good moment, but any time from November to March is suitable, which is when bare-root trees are sold. Pot-grown trees can go in at other times, but they settle best from late autumn to spring, and not in heat and drought. A south or south-west wall or fence is best, so the tree can be fan-trained. Free-standing trees need a stout stake until established. Compact kinds can be grown in pots. Because they flower early, avoid frost-prone sites – covering blossom is practical mainly on fans and pot trees.

**Spacing:** A fan needs a wide stretch of wall, typically 11–16ft (3.5–5m), and is planted about 8–12in (20–30cm) out from the base (other training notes mention widths around 6½–16ft / 2–5m). A pot should be at least 18in (45cm) wide.

**Care:** Water new trees in dry spells for the first two years; fans at the foot of a wall, pot trees, and glasshouse trees need extra moisture, especially while fruit is swelling in early to mid-summer. Established free-standing trees rarely need watering. Mulch, feed, protect blossom from frost, help with pollination if needed, and thin the crop. Cover against peach leaf curl as recommended. Prune annually.

**Harvest window:** July–September, when fruit is fully coloured and the flesh by the stalk feels soft. Cup it and lift gently – it should part easily. Check often, as fruit does not all ripen together.

**Storing:** Best eaten as soon as possible, ideally straight from the tree. Fruit picked a little early will finish indoors, but flavour is not quite as good. A long store is not described.

**Common problems:** Bacterial canker, brown rot, brown scale, peach leaf curl, fruit aphids, grey squirrels, silver leaf, spotted wing drosophila, wasps.

**Varieties:**

- **'Nectarella'** (recommended)
- **'Humboldt'** (recommended)

### Olives

**Sow:** Bought as plants, from small half-standards to large specimen trees, not usually raised from seed.

**Plant out:** Spring (March–May) is the time to plant in the ground, choosing free-draining ground in a sheltered, sunny place beside a wall, and stake until established. In cool climates they often do better in large pots that stand in a sunny spot for summer and move to a frost-free place for winter.

**Spacing:** A pot of 12–14in (30–35cm), or large enough to take the roots comfortably, filled with a gritty, loam-based compost, with some controlled-release fertiliser mixed in. In-ground spacing is not stated as a figure; allow for the tree's eventual size.

**Care:** In the growing season keep compost moist and give a balanced liquid feed each month. Prune in early spring to keep a good shape and take out dead or damaged wood; on pot trees, tip-prune main branches back to a strong replacement shoot if you need to limit size. Many olives are hardy, but severe frost can still damage branches – store pot plants frost-free, or wrap branches in horticultural fleece, and stand pots on feet so surplus water can drain. Do not move pot plants out for summer until frost risk has passed.

**Harvest window:** September–October. Ripe green fruit later turns black and firm on the tree.

**Storing:** Green olives need several days in salted water to take off the bitterness. Black olives are packed in dry salt for some weeks until the moisture has gone, then kept in olive oil or brine.

**Common problems:** No particular problems are listed.

### Pears

**Sow:** Bought as named trees on a chosen rootstock (Quince A, or the more dwarfing Quince C or Quince Eline), not usually raised from seed. A second, compatible variety nearby is often needed for pollination.

**Plant out:** While dormant, autumn to spring (calendar October–April, also January–March). Pot-grown trees can go in at other times, but not in heat and drought, or into frozen or waterlogged ground. A sunny, sheltered site – open ground or trained on a wall – away from late frosts that can ruin blossom.

**Spacing:** In grass, clear at least 3¼ft (1m) around the trunk. Free-standing trees 11–18ft (3.5–5.5m) apart on Quince A, or 8–11ft (2.5–3.5m) on Quince C or Eline. Trained trees about 1ft (30cm) out from the wall: espaliers and fans at least 12ft (3.6m) apart, cordons about 30in (75cm). A pot should be 18–20in (45–50cm) wide and deep, heavy enough not to blow over, on a dwarfing stock such as Quince Eline.

**Care:** Water new trees through dry weather for the first two years; pot trees need even moisture in the growing months. Established trees in the ground rarely need water except in severe drought while fruit is swelling. Mulch each spring. On trained trees, thin to one fruit per cluster, 4–6in (10–15cm) apart; on reachable free-standing trees, thin to two per cluster. Prune according to form.

**Harvest window:** August–October. Pick just before the fruit is fully ripe, when it is firm and swollen and the skin has begun to change colour. Early kinds can be tested by tasting one for sweetness; later kinds should lift and twist off easily. A long-handled picker is safer than a tall ladder.

**Storing:** Pears improve if ripened after picking: early kinds often need about a week, later ones may take months. Keep unripe fruit in a cool, dark, airy place (for example a garage) on slatted trays. They are ready to eat when they yield slightly, especially around the stalk.

**Common problems:** Apple canker, apple scab and pear scab, biennial bearing, brown rot, pear rust, fruit aphids, pear blister mite, pear midge, wasps.

**Varieties:**

- **'Beth' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Beurre Hardy' AGM** (recommended) — Red Beurré Hardy is a medium, conical or pyriform pear; where the original Beurré Hardy is greenish-yellow and heavily russeted, this form has dull red skin instead. It is best as a dessert pear eaten fresh, and sits well alongside pecans, walnuts and similar nuts. The first Beurré Hardy was grown in 1820 by M. Bonnet at Boulogne, in northern France, and named after the director of arboriculture at the Luxembourg gardens.
- **'Beurre Superfin' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Concorde' AGM** (recommended) — Concorde has a large, round base tapering into a long, narrow, pointed neck, with smooth green-yellow skin, some red blush and golden-brown russet. The white to ivory flesh is dense and firm, good raw or poached, baked, grilled and sautéed. It is an English pear, first bred in 1977 at East Malling in Kent.
- **'Conference' AGM** (recommended) — Conference is a distinctly elongated pear, typically 1¾–3 inches (4.5–8 cm) across, often larger than many European market types, with a bulbous, curved base tapering to a long neck. The flavour is sweet, fruity and floral, used fresh or cooked, and the fruit can be eaten at different stages of ripeness. The English nurseryman Thomas Francis Rivers raised it in the 19th century as an open-pollinated seedling at his nursery in Sawbridgeworth.
- **'Doyenne du Comice' AGM** (recommended) — Doyenné du Comice is a large, irregular pear, typically 2¾–4 inches (7–10 cm) across, squat and bulbous, tapering to a rounded neck, with delicate yellow-green skin that bruises easily and is patched with brown russet. It is at its best eaten fresh, when the sweet flavour can be enjoyed raw. It was first raised in the mid-19th century in the grounds of the Comice Horticole at Angers, in the Loire valley.
- **'Gorham' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Josephine de Malines' AGM** (recommended)
- **Williams** — Williams is a classic pyriform pear, medium to large, with a rounded base tapering to a curved neck; the thin skin ripens from green to golden yellow. Use it raw, or bake, boil and grill it. It was found in England in 1765 by a schoolmaster named Stair, then taken on and distributed by a nurseryman called Williams.

Its exact origin is unknown, but it is thought to have reached Britain with the Romans and was recorded by monks at Warden Abbey in Bedfordshire as early as 1388.

Use it raw, or bake and poach it. It comes from just north of Brussels, introduced by the amateur horticulturist Jean-Charles Nelis.

### Quince

**Sow:** Bought as named trees on a chosen rootstock, not usually raised from seed.

**Plant out:** While dormant, November–March. A warm, sunny, sheltered site, as the spring flowers are easily damaged by frost and sun is needed for fruit to ripen. In southern England, or in mild urban or coastal spots, trees often crop well in the open; further north or in colder, more exposed places, a south or south-west wall gives extra warmth. They prefer deep, fertile, moisture-holding soil but tolerate a range of types.

**Spacing:** Allow for an eventual 10–15ft (3–4.5m) in height and spread, according to rootstock, site and soil. A pot should be about 18in–2ft (45–60cm) wide if you grow a compact tree in a container.

**Care:** Water new trees through dry weather for the first two years; pot trees need even moisture in the growing months. Established trees in moisture-holding soil rarely need extra water, except on free-draining ground or in long droughts while fruit is swelling. Mulch each spring, feed if needed, and protect blossom from frost. Prune as recommended.

**Harvest window:** October–November, when fruit has turned from light yellow to gold and is very aromatic. It seldom ripens fully in UK summers, so leave it on the tree as long as you reasonably can, then pick before the first frost.

**Storing:** Store only sound fruit, unwrapped and not touching, in slatted wooden or cardboard trays in a cool, dark place, leaving them at least six or eight weeks so they can mellow. They will keep two or three months. They are not eaten raw; use them in jams, jellies, pies, tarts and quince paste (membrillo).

**Common problems:** Aphids, brown rot, fireblight, codling moth, powdery mildews, quince leaf blight.

**In the kitchen:** Quinces are lumpy, pear-shaped fruits, typically 2½–5 inches (6–13 cm) tall and 2½–3½ inches (6–9 cm) wide, bright yellow-green, sometimes with faint lime streaks and a little russet. They are tough, sour and astringent, so they are almost always cooked; their high pectin makes them excellent for jellies and pastes. Their homeland is western Asia and the Caucasus, with origins thought to lie in Iran and Turkestan.

**Varieties:**

- **'Meech's Prolific'** (recommended)
- **'Vranja'** (recommended)
- **'Serbian Gold' AGM** (recommended)

### Raspberries

**Sow:** Planted as canes, not usually raised from seed. Do not replant in ground that has recently grown raspberries, to avoid replant problems. Choose a new site if you are replacing an old row.

**Plant out:** Late autumn is a good time, but any time while dormant, November–March, provided the ground is not frozen (calendar November–March, also January–February). Fertile, free-draining soil on the slightly acid side (around pH 6 to 6.7); they dislike thin chalk and sitting wet in winter – raised beds can help drainage. Full sun gives the best crop; light shade is tolerated but yields and vigour suffer.

**Spacing:** Canes 16–24in (40–60cm) apart, with 5–6½ft (1.5–2m) between rows, according to variety. Set the uppermost roots no more than 2in (5cm) below the surface. After planting, apply a 2–3in (5–7.5cm) mulch, keeping it off the stems, and (except for summer 'long canes') cut stems down to 10in (25cm). Compact kinds can go in a pot at least 16in (40cm) wide and 12in (30cm) deep, using a mix of roughly two parts peat-free multi-purpose compost to one part loam-based compost. Posts about 6ft (1.8m) with wires are a typical support.

**Care:** Water well at planting and through dry weather in the first season; established plants rarely need extra water, though shallow roots may need a drink in a long drought while fruit is swelling. Keep pot compost consistently moist, using rainwater if you can, especially in hard-water areas. Mulch each year, tie in canes, thin congested growth, and prune summer-fruiting and autumn-fruiting types differently (autumn kinds can also be managed for a smaller early crop plus the main autumn one). Remove unwanted suckers. Plants may crop well for up to ten years.

**Harvest window:** June–October. Early summer types may be ready from June, later ones into September; autumn types start in August and often run well into October. Pick often, on a dry day, when berries are richly coloured, plump and come away easily. Use a shallow punnet, only two or three layers deep, so they are not crushed.

**Storing:** Best eaten fresh. A glut freezes well and makes good jam, sauces and puddings.

**Common problems:** Nutrient deficiencies, grey mould, raspberry cane blight, raspberry rust, raspberry spur blight, raspberry viruses, raspberry and blackberry aphids, raspberry beetle, raspberry leaf and bud mite.

**In the kitchen:** Raspberries are small aggregate fruits, typically ⅜–1¼ inches (1–3 cm) across, made of tiny drupelets fused into a bumpy, hollow berry, with a sweet-tart, tangy flavour used in both sweet and savoury cooking, or eaten fresh. They are an ancient wild crop of several temperate regions, including Europe.

**Varieties:**

- **'All Gold' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Octavia' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Glen Ample' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Glen Magna' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Joan J' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Malling Admiral' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Malling Jewel' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Polka' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Tulameen' AGM** (recommended)
- **Golden raspberry** — Golden raspberries are the same kind of small, plump, aggregate fruit, typically ⅜–1¼ inches (1–3 cm) across, but milder and sweeter, with low acidity, used mainly in fresh and cooked sweet dishes. Like red raspberries they are an ancient temperate crop, with many varieties in Europe and elsewhere.

### Redcurrants

**Sow:** Usually planted as bushes or trained plants; hardwood cuttings about 8in (20cm) long, taken from mid-autumn to late winter, are a common way to increase stock.

**Plant out:** November–March is best, even for pot-grown bushes, though these can go in at other times. Full sun is preferred, but partial shade – including a north-facing wall – is acceptable, with later, less sweet fruit. Choose a sheltered spot away from late frosts that can spoil flowers. A fruit cage helps against birds. They also suit a raised bed.

**Spacing:** Bushes 4–5ft (1.2–1.5m) apart; cordons 12–16in (30–40cm); fans 5ft (1.5m). A pot should be at least 18in (45cm) wide, filled with a soil-based peat-free mix such as John Innes No. 3. Horizontal wires for trained forms are usually set at 2ft (60cm) intervals.

**Care:** Water new plants in dry spells for the first two years; established bushes in the ground rarely need routine watering, but pots should stay evenly moist. Mulch each spring. Prune annually. Net, or use a fruit cage, once fruit begins to colour.

**Harvest window:** July–August, from early July to late August according to early, mid and late varieties. Pick when currants are richly coloured, firm and juicy, cutting whole strigs (trusses) and stripping them in the kitchen. A mature bush may give about 10lb (4.5kg).

**Storing:** Use fresh in puddings, or make jelly – they are high in pectin. A long fresh store is not spelled out.

**Common problems:** Currant and gooseberry leaf spot, brown scale, capsid bugs, gooseberry mildew, grey mould, coral spot, currant aphids, gooseberry sawfly, woolly vine or currant scale.

**In the kitchen:** Redcurrants are small, typically ⅓–½ inch (8–12 mm) across, round to oval, with smooth, glossy, delicate skin that punctures easily, and a sweet-sour flavour used fresh or cooked. They must be picked by hand. They are a wild plant of Europe and Asia, with the densest populations thought to have been in Europe.

**Varieties:**

- **'Jonkheer van Tets' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Red Lake' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Rovada' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Stanza' AGM** (recommended)

### Strawberries

**Sow:** Ordinary summer and perpetual (everbearing) strawberries are started from runners or young plants. Alpine types can also be raised from seed, sown in spring or autumn on peat-free seed compost, thinly, with a light covering of sharp sand, at about 64°F (18°C). Germination can be slow and uneven. Prick out into small pots and move to the final spot once well rooted; they often fruit in the first year.

**Plant out:** Ideally in spring, late summer or early autumn (calendar March–June and August–October). August or September plantings have time to settle before cropping the next year. Cold-stored runners, sold from spring to early summer, should go in at once. Fertile, free-draining, slightly acidic soil; on poor or heavy ground, raised beds help. They also grow well as bed edging, or in containers of various kinds – large pots, growing-bags, hanging baskets or window boxes – which avoids soil diseases building up in a permanent patch (though vine weevil can then be a risk).

**Spacing:** In the ground, 14–16in (35–40cm) apart, in rows about 30in (75cm) apart, with the base of the crown level with the soil surface – too deep and it may rot, too shallow and the plant dries out. Trim bare-root roots to 4in (10cm) if needed and spread them in the hole. In a container, about 8in (20cm) apart, crown just level with the compost, in a sheltered sunny position. New plants from runners can be rooted into 3½in (9cm) pots pegged down with 6in (15cm) wire staples.

**Care:** Keep weeds down. Water new plants until they settle, and all plants in long dry spells; pot and basket plants dry quickly. Avoid wetting the crown and fruit, which invites grey mould, and water in the morning so any splash can dry. Mulch, and feed only if crops are poor, with a high-potash organic fertiliser. Remove flowers from newly planted runners if you are waiting for a stronger later crop (cold-stored runners, given good conditions, may fruit about 60 days after planting, then follow their usual season the next year). Protect flowers from frost (black eye prevents fruit set) and ripening fruit from birds and slugs. After harvest, pot up spare runners to fill gaps or replace old plants, and remove the rest.

**Harvest window:** June–October, according to variety, from early summer into autumn. Pick when berries are bright red all over, ideally in the warmest part of the day, when flavour is at its best. They will not ripen further once picked.

**Storing:** Eat as soon as possible – ripe fruit does not keep long. They can be frozen, though they usually collapse on thawing, and are then still useful in smoothies and puddings.

**Common problems:** Phyllody, strawberry black eye, grey mould, fruit aphids, strawberry viruses, verticillium wilt, slugs and snails, spotted wing drosophila, vine weevil.

**In the kitchen:** Garden strawberries are small to medium, typically ¾–2 inches (2–5 cm) across, plump and heart-shaped, varying in size, scent and flavour by cultivar, generally sweet, tart and fruity, used fresh or cooked. Modern market types descend from wild species that have grown since antiquity; experts think some five or six wild species contributed to the cultivated crop.

**Varieties:**

- **'Florence' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Finesse' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Cambridge Favourite' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Hapil' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Honeoye' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Rhapsody' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Pegasus' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Symphony' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Vibrant' AGM** (recommended)
- **Mara des Bois strawberry** — Mara des Bois is a smaller, round to slightly conical strawberry, typically ⅜–½ oz (11–16 g), with a sweet, fruity, floral flavour used fresh or cooked, and a reputation for a particularly fine aroma. It is a French variety, raised in 1991 by the fruit breeder Jacques Marionnet at Soings-en-Sologne.
- **White alpine strawberry** — White alpine strawberries are tiny cream-yellow or greenish-white fruits on short plants about 6 inches (15 cm) tall that usually do not run. They are as versatile as red types but are at their best eaten raw, so the flavour is not lost. They are a natural mutation of the wild red wood strawberry, first noticed some three centuries ago to the east of Grenoble, in the lower Alps.

### Whitecurrants

**Sow:** Usually planted as bushes or trained plants; cuttings are a common way to increase stock, as for redcurrants.

**Plant out:** November–March is best, when the ground is neither frozen nor waterlogged. Pot-grown bushes can go in at other times but settle better while dormant. Full sun gives the best crop; light shade, including a north-facing wall, is tolerated, with later, less sweet berries. Choose a sheltered site away from late frosts that can spoil flowers. **Spacing:** Bushes 4–5ft (1.2–1.5m) apart; cordons 12–16in (30–40cm); fans 5ft (1.5m). A pot should be at least 18in (45cm) wide, filled with a soil-based compost. Horizontal wires for trained forms are usually set at 2ft (60cm) intervals.

**Care:** Water new plants in dry spells for the first two years; established plants in the ground rarely need routine watering, but pots should stay evenly moist. Mulch each spring. Prune annually. Net, or use a fruit cage, once fruit begins to colour.

**Harvest window:** July–August, from early July to late August according to variety. On modern cultivars such as 'Blanka' and 'White Grape', a whole strig colours together and can be cut as a truss, then stripped in the kitchen; older kinds ripen from the top of the strig first, so pick berries individually when they are sweet, firm and juicy. A mature bush may give about 10lb (4.5kg) a year.

**Storing:** Eat raw, or cook for jams and summer puddings. A long fresh store is not spelled out.

**Common problems:** Currant and gooseberry leaf spot, brown scale, capsid bugs, gooseberry mildew, grey mould, coral spot, currant aphids, gooseberry sawfly, woolly vine or currant scale.

**In the kitchen:** Whitecurrants are small, round berries, typically ¼–⅜ inch (0.5–1 cm) across, looking rather like tiny green grapes, ripening from green to translucent pale gold, in strands on shrubs that can reach about 6½ feet (2 m). They can be eaten fresh, cooked, dried, or frosted with caster sugar as a garnish. They are a natural mutation of the redcurrant, with ancestry in the centre and east of Europe and in parts of Asia.

## Herbs

### Basil

**Sow:** Usually started under cover from March to July, for earlier, surer germination and to keep slugs off the seedlings. Scatter seed thinly on damp peat-free seed mix; once seedlings have several leaves, move them into 3in (7.5cm) pots, or several to a larger pot 3in (7.5cm) apart. Outdoors only in warmer districts, and not until early summer, because basil dislikes cold, damp weather. Thin outdoor seedlings to 4–8in (10–20cm). Short sowings a few weeks apart stretch the harvest. Seedlings can also be eaten after a couple of weeks as microgreens.

**Plant out:** Early summer (June–July), after hardening off, in a warm, sunny, sheltered spot. It usually does best in a pot, but will also grow in well-drained soil. Plant at the same depth as before.

**Spacing:** At least 4in (10cm) apart. A pot should be at least 8in (20cm) wide and deep, filled with peat-free compost. Plants typically stand 8–20in (20–50cm) tall if kept bushy by picking.

**Care:** Needs little attention. In a pot, keep the compost slightly damp and water at the base, preferably early in the day, without wetting the foliage. Outdoor plants usually only need watering in dry spells. Once they begin to flower, tip-prune regularly to keep them leafy. Bring pot plants indoors before the first frost. Feed every fortnight in the growing season with a high-potash organic liquid fertiliser.

**Harvest window:** June–October, through the summer. Take a few shoot tips from each plant as needed, or cut whole plants if you need a bulk of leaves for pesto. The flowers are edible too, with a milder flavour.

**Storing:** Leaves are used fresh, and seedlings can be eaten as microgreens; no long store is given.

**Common problems:** Aphids, damping off, grey mould, powdery mildew, sage and Ligurian leafhoppers, slugs and snails.

**Varieties:**

- **'Salvo' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Mrs Burns' Lemon' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Pluto' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Aroma 2' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Thai Sita'** (recommended)
- **Red-leaved AGM** (recommended)
- **Dark Opal basil** — Dark Opal basil has small to medium, pointed oval leaves, typically 1–3 inches (3–7 cm) long, smooth but crinkled and serrated, in variegated dark-purple shades, with a fragrant, mild flavour used fresh or cooked. Breeders at Connecticut’s state university raised it in the 1950s from wild Turkish seed supplied by the USDA.
- **Thai basil** — Thai basil is a perennial herb about 12–18 inches (30–45 cm) tall and a foot (30 cm) across, with narrow, serrated, bright-green leaves that are smaller and tougher than sweet basil, and a fresh, spicy scent. It is used fresh or cooked in Thai, Cambodian, Lao and Vietnamese dishes. It is a south-east Asian plant, cultivated for perhaps 5,000 years, though the precise origin is debated.

### Bay

**Sow:** Not usually raised from seed; start from plants.

**Plant out:** Spring (March–May) is best in the open ground, once the soil is warming, so plants have a full season to settle before winter. Choose a warm, sheltered spot in full sun or light shade, with free-draining soil that does not lie wet. Poor soil can be improved with garden compost at planting. It also grows well in a pot.

**Spacing:** In the ground it can eventually reach 33ft (10m) or more, but is easily kept smaller by pruning. Exact pot sizes are not specified.

**Care:** Low maintenance once settled. Water new plants in dry spells through the first year; established plants in the ground rarely need watering, but pot plants need regular watering and feeding. In colder districts it may need winter protection. Clip annually for a neat shape, or prune harder to reduce size. Trim long shoots and topiary in summer, preferably with secateurs or loppers rather than shears, which leave unsightly cut leaf edges. On standard (lollipop) trees, pull out basal suckers to keep a single clear stem.

**Harvest window:** All year, as it is evergreen. Pick a few leaves as needed. Newly gathered leaves taste slightly more bitter and punchy than dried ones, which mellow in storage.

**Storing:** Leaves can be used fresh or dried. To dry them, lay sprigs or single leaves in a warm room.

**Common problems:** Frost damage, bay sucker, scale insects.

**Varieties:**

- **Willow-leaved bay AGM** (recommended)
- **Golden bay AGM** (recommended)
- **Bay AGM** (recommended)

### Chamomile

**Sow:** Both common (Roman) and German chamomile are easy from seed, under cover or outdoors, in March–May, and can flower in as little as ten weeks. Under cover, scatter seed on the surface of damp peat-free seed mix – it needs light to germinate – then pot on when large enough, keeping plants warm, bright and just moist. Thin outdoor seedlings to 6in (15cm).

**Plant out:** Late spring and early summer (May–June), after hardening off, in sun on light, well-drained soil that neither dries out completely nor lies wet. Plant at the same depth as before and water in. Dwarf types for a chamomile lawn go 4in (10cm) apart.

**Spacing:** About 6in (15cm) apart for ordinary plantings; 4in (10cm) for dwarf lawn types.

**Care:** Little work once settled: water in dry spells so plants do not dry out, and clip lightly if they grow leggy. The more flowers you pick, the more they produce. A chamomile lawn needs regular weeding and will not stand heavy foot traffic like grass. Clumps can be divided and replanted at once.

**Harvest window:** July–September. Pick newly opened flowers through the summer; regular picking and deadheading prolongs the display. Use them fresh for tea, or dry them.

**Storing:** Spread flowers in a single layer on a tray somewhere warm and dry, out of direct sun. Dried flowers can be kept for up to a year, and also used in pot-pourri and herbal pillows.

**Common problems:** Aphids, slugs and snails.

**Varieties:**

- **'Flore pleno'** (recommended)
- **'Treneague'** (recommended)

### Chervil

**Sow:** Quick from seed, under cover or outdoors. Sow short batches through spring and summer (March–August) for a steady supply of young leaves; seed stays useful for about a year. Outdoors, thinly in a ½in (1cm) drill, rows 12in (30cm) apart, thinned to 6in (15cm). Under cover, from early spring on a sunny sill or in a glasshouse, keep the compost slightly moist. In a pot at least 10in (25cm) wide and deep, cover seed with about ½in (1cm) of peat-free compost.

**Plant out:** From mid-spring (April–June) once plants are about 2in (5cm) tall and before the long tap root develops, after hardening off. Plant at the same depth, with as little root disturbance as possible, in light shade, especially in summer – hot sun can cause bolting.

**Spacing:** 6in (15cm) after thinning, rows 12in (30cm) apart. Pot at least 10in (25cm) wide and deep.

**Care:** Little work beyond watering in dry weather to delay bolting. Keep the soil or compost evenly moist, never waterlogged. Once plants flower the leaves are past their best – remove them, or leave a few to set seed for next year.

**Harvest window:** May–November, typically about eight weeks after sowing, once plants are at least 4in (10cm) tall. Take a few soft young leaves from each plant, or, for a bulk picking, cut all stems back to 2–3in (5–7.5cm); healthy plants should throw a new flush.

**Storing:** Used fresh; no long store is described.

**Common problems:** Bolting, aphids.

**In the kitchen:** Chervil is a delicate herb, typically about 12 inches (30 cm) tall, sometimes to 2 feet (60 cm), with light-green, lacy leaves on thin hollow stems, rather like a frillier flat parsley or carrot top. Cooks usually add it fresh; in France it is a fixture of the fines herbes mix, especially with fish and poultry. It is native to the Caucasus and was carried through Europe by the Romans.

**Varieties:**

- **Chervil** (recommended)

### Chives

**Sow:** Seed can be sown under cover in early spring, a few seeds ¼–½in (0.5–1cm) deep in modules or small pots of peat-free seed mix, in a warm bright place, for planting at about 4in (10cm) tall in late spring or early summer. Later spring and summer sowings can supply a windowsill crop. Potted plants are cheap and widely sold.

**Plant out:** Bought plants can go out at any time, though spring is ideal (calendar plant-out April–June). Large clumps can be pulled into several smaller ones. Seed-raised plants go out at about 4in (10cm) tall; harden off if moving them out before June.

**Spacing:** A precise in-ground distance is not stated. Leaves typically stand about 12in (30cm) tall; garlic chives 16–20in (40–50cm); Siberian types up to 2ft (60cm).

**Care:** Straightforward once established. Keep clumps from becoming overcrowded by splitting them at three- to four-year intervals, ideally in spring or autumn. Water in dry weather and snip regularly to encourage fresh leaves. Remove spent flower heads if you do not want seedlings.

**Harvest window:** May–November outdoors, from late spring until the leaves die back in autumn. Snip leaves just above the base, preferably in the morning, and remove any faded ones. Flowers are edible. For a winter supply, pot a clump and keep it on a sunny indoor sill.

**Storing:** Used fresh; bringing a pot indoors is a reliable way to keep a winter supply.

**Common problems:** Aphids, leek rust.

**In the kitchen:** Chives form dense clumps of hollow, pencil-thin, pointed green leaves, with spherical, spiky purple flowers on tougher stems. They belong to the onion family and taste similar but more delicate, and are best used fresh as a garnish — baked potatoes, bagels, soups and salads. It is thought to have arisen independently in China and in Europe, with records back to about 3000 BCE in the Balkans, Siberia and China; Marco Polo is often credited with spreading it more widely.

**Varieties:**

- **Siberian chives** (recommended)
- **Garlic chives** (recommended) — Garlic chives have flat, ribbed green leaves, typically 6–15 inches (15–38 cm) long and about 2–3 inches (5–8 cm) wide, firm enough to keep their shape when cut, each stem ending in a tiny white bulb that is not eaten. Use them raw or cooked; they often appear in pad Thai and in other noodle dishes. They have been grown in south-western China for more than 4,000 years.
- **Chives** (recommended)

### Coriander

**Sow:** Best sown direct outdoors, because it dislikes being moved and can grow leggy if kept indoors too long (calendar February–October). Space the large seeds about 2in (5cm) apart in a shallow drill, or on firmed compost in pots or growing bags; thin to 2in (5cm) at first, then to 8in (20cm) if you want plants to mature. Short-term indoor pots and microgreens are useful if you have no outdoor space, from February onwards. Sow short batches for a steady supply.

**Plant out:** Bought plants from spring (April–June), after hardening off, especially if they have been under glass. Protect from slugs, and use cloches or biodegradable fleece if it is still cold. Plant at the same depth as before.

**Spacing:** About 4in (10cm) for bought plants; seed thinned to 2in (5cm), or 8in (20cm) for mature plants.

**Care:** A short-term, fast crop needing little work. In the ground, water only in long dry spells, which helps delay bolting and lengthen the leaf harvest. In pots, keep the compost just moist, checking daily, and do not overwater – it dislikes soggy conditions.

**Harvest window:** April–December. Leaves, flowers and seeds are all edible. Start cutting leaves as a cut-and-come-again crop when plants are 4–6in (10–15cm) tall, taking only a few from each plant and waiting a couple of weeks between cuts; several pickings are usual before flowering. Regular picking encourages more leaves.

**Storing:** Fresh leafy stems keep for a few days in a jar of water in the fridge, loosely covered. Leaves can also be frozen or dried, though flavour is milder. Seed heads are dried and then threshed for the coriander seed.

**Common problems:** Bolting, aphids, slugs and snails.

**In the kitchen:** Coriander leaf, also sold as cilantro, has long slender stems bearing dark-green, broad, deeply lobed, feathery leaves, larger at the base and more delicate at the tips. The herb is used torn or chopped as a finishing garnish in savoury dishes. Experts regard it as an ancient plant of southern Europe and Asia Minor, used for both leaves and seed from early times.

**Varieties:**

### Dill

**Sow:** Best started in the open ground from about April through to July, because it resents root disturbance. Sow thinly about ½in (1cm) deep, in short drills 12in (30cm) apart, and thin later. A mid-summer sowing gives a later leaf crop. Under cover, modules can be used if you will plant them on promptly without disturbing the roots.

**Plant out:** April–July if you started plants in modules, after hardening off, choosing a bright, open position on free-draining ground. One plant to a pot at least 12in (30cm) wide and deep also works well.

**Spacing:** About 8in (20cm) after thinning, rows 12in (30cm) apart. Pot at least 12in (30cm) wide and deep. Plants may reach 5ft (1.5m) but often flower and stop at about 3ft (90cm).

**Care:** Water in dry spells – heat and drought cause bolting, which ends leaf production. Pots dry quickly in warm weather. Protect seedlings from slugs. Regular picking keeps plants bushy and productive.

**Harvest window:** May–October. Start taking leafy shoots once plants are well grown, always leaving plenty of foliage so the plant can recover. Flower heads can be used fresh, or left to form seed. Successional sowings keep a leaf supply going.

**Storing:** Seeds can be saved once brown; otherwise the leaves are used fresh.

**Common problems:** Bolting, aphids, slugs and snails.

**In the kitchen:** Dill is a delicate, fragrant annual typically about 3 feet (90 cm) tall, sometimes to 4 feet (120 cm), with slender stems and soft, feathery, fern-like leaves, and later umbrella-like flower heads. It is best used fresh as a garnish or flavouring, and is especially popular in eastern European, Russian and Scandinavian cooking. It comes from Mediterranean and west-Asian countries, and the earliest written mention is from ancient Egypt.

**Varieties:**

- **'Domino' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Dukat'** (recommended)
- **'Mammoth'** (recommended)

### Fennel

**Sow:** Best sown in the final spot in mid- to late spring (April–May), because it resents root disturbance. Sow ½in (1cm) deep, then thin gradually to at least 1ft (30cm); thinnings can go in salads. Under cover, one seed per module, ½in (1cm) deep, kept in good light and watered regularly. This is herb fennel (common or bronze), not Florence fennel grown for bulbs.

**Plant out:** Bought plants in late spring and early summer (May–June). Move indoor seedlings while still very young, before the tap root forms, after hardening off, at the same depth, without disturbing the roots, and water in. A large pot at least 1ft (30cm) wide and deep of peat-free compost also works.

**Spacing:** At least 1ft (30cm) from other plants.

**Care:** Little work once settled: no routine feed, and established plants in the ground are drought-tolerant and need water only in pots, or while new plants settle in their first dry spells. In wind, cane the tall flower stems. Remove old stems before new growth in spring, or cut the whole plant back to about 1ft (30cm) several times in the season for a supply of young leaves.

**Harvest window:** June–October. Leaves, flowers and seeds all have a warm aniseed flavour, for tea, salads or cooking. Pick young leaf sprigs from early summer to autumn. Seeds can be used fresh in summer or dried: cut whole heads in late summer when they ripen, hang in a paper bag until the seed falls, then store.

**Storing:** Dry seedheads in a paper bag and keep the seed for later use.

**Common problems:** Aphids, slugs and snails.

**Varieties:**

- **Common fennel** (recommended)
- **Bronze fennel** (recommended) — Bronze fennel is a tall herb, up to about 6 feet (1.8 m), with green stems flushed maroon and fine, feathery leaves in blue-green and bronze, plus plentiful tiny flowers in umbels. The seeds go into breads, biscuits and Italian sausages, and the foliage pairs well with other vegetables. It is commonly grown in Britain and the United States; all fennels are ancient plants, used since classical times.

### Horseradish

**Sow:** Seed is not widely sold; most plants are started from root sections (thongs) or young plants, which is quicker. If you have seed, sow it in a pot outdoors in spring, not in open ground, because the deep roots are hard to remove. Use a container at least 12in (30cm) wide and deep, in a warm sunny spot, filled with peat-free compost.

**Plant out:** Spring or early summer (March–June), from potted plants or long root sections, in a large, deep pot in sun or partial shade. Open-ground planting is not recommended – clumps spread vigorously and regrow from any root left behind. To plant a thong, make a deep hole, set the root vertically with the top 2in (5cm) below the surface, cover and water.

**Spacing:** A pot at least 12in (30cm) wide and deep. Clumps can reach 35in (90cm) tall.

**Care:** Once established it needs little attention, but it is invasive, so a pot is often safer than open ground. Keep the compost moist in the growing season. After the top dies in autumn, you can leave the roots in the pot until late winter, or lift them before growth resumes in spring.

**Harvest window:** September–December, preferably in autumn or early winter, when frost is said to strengthen the flavour. Roots can also be taken in spring and summer, but the disturbed plant may need extra water to resettle in drier months. New plants can be picked over lightly in their second autumn. Tip the pot or ease roots out, then wash, peel and grate fresh.

**Storing:** Use freshly grated after washing and peeling. A long indoor store is not described.

**Common problems:** No particular problems are listed.

**In the kitchen:** Horseradish is a tapered, knobbly tan root, typically 2–8 inches (5–20 cm) long and ¾–2 inches (2–5 cm) across, with dense, juicy white flesh that tastes deceptively mild until it is grated and the familiar fierce heat rises. It is used almost always raw, mixed with vinegar as a sauce or relish, because long cooking dulls the punch. It comes from the east of Europe — Hungary and Russia in particular — was being grown in England by the 1600s, and travelled to America with settlers in the 1800s.

**Varieties:**

- **Armoracia rusticana** (recommended)

### Lemon balm

**Sow:** Easy from a few seeds in spring (March–June), under cover or direct, in the ground or in a pot – one or two plants are usually enough, and established plants self-sow freely. Under cover, barely cover seed with fine compost in a small pot or module of peat-free seed mix.

**Plant out:** Bought plants can go out at any time, though spring is ideal (calendar March–May). Seed-raised plants from late spring, after hardening off, in sun or partial shade. Most soils suit it if they do not lie wet. A pot at least 10in (25cm) wide and deep of peat-free soil-based compost is useful if you do not want it to spread.

**Spacing:** One or two plants usually suffice. Pot at least 10in (25cm) wide and deep. Flower stems can reach 3ft (90cm).

**Care:** Little work: cut back after flowering, especially if you do not want self-sown seedlings, and divide every few years so clumps do not become unruly. Pot plants need the compost kept evenly moist; established plants in the ground rarely need watering. Cutting back after flowering also brings a flush of new, tasty leaves. Plants die back naturally as temperatures fall in autumn.

**Harvest window:** April–October. Pick the youngest leaves whenever needed through spring and summer, and use them as soon as you can, in savoury or sweet dishes or as a tea.

**Storing:** Best used fresh; a long store is not described.

**Common problems:** Powdery mildew, sage and Ligurian leafhoppers.

**In the kitchen:** Lemon balm is a bushy perennial about 2 feet (60 cm) tall and wide, with wrinkled, jagged, heart-shaped leaves on square stems and, later, small white or yellow nectar-rich flowers, and a strong lemon scent. Use the leaves fresh in fruit or green salads, or dry them for rubs and seasoning. Its home range is southern Europe and the north of Africa, and it was already in use in classical Greece and Rome.

**Varieties:**

- **'Aurea'** (recommended)
- **Lemon balm** (recommended)
- **'All Gold'** (recommended)

### Lemongrass

**Sow:** Buying young plants in spring is the quickest start, but seed can be sown under cover in early to mid-spring (March–April). Scatter thinly on peat-free seed mix, dust lightly with fine compost, and germinate at 68–77°F (20–25°C) on a heated bench or bright warm windowsill; seedlings usually appear in two to three weeks.

**Plant out:** Early summer (calendar June), after careful hardening off. These tender plants need at least 55°F (13°C) to thrive; in cooler districts keep them in a warm, bright conservatory or glasshouse all year. A pot makes it easy to move them indoors for winter. Wear gloves when handling mature leaves, which can be sharp.

**Spacing:** At least 1ft (30cm) apart if you grow more than one. Plants can reach 5ft (1.5m) tall and 3ft (1m) wide, but are unlikely to get that large in a UK pot.

**Care:** Little work if they have a sunny, sheltered summer spot and winter warmth indoors. Keep pot compost evenly moist, checking often in warm weather. In the ground, water until well rooted, then only in dry spells. For winter, move pots under cover; if indoor space is tight, cut all leaves back to 2–4in (5–10cm) in autumn, or do this in early spring as new growth starts.

**Harvest window:** May–September, through the growing season. Take outer stems with swollen bases, cutting or pulling them as low as you can. Trim off the upper leaf and keep only the lower 4in (10cm) of stem, which has the best flavour, then slice or chop it for cooking.

**Storing:** The lower stem is used fresh; no long store is given.

**Common problems:** Glasshouse whitefly, rust diseases.

**Varieties:**

- **Lemon grass** (recommended)
- **East Indian lemon grass** (recommended)

### Lovage

**Sow:** Sow a few seeds only in spring (March–May) – one plant is usually enough. Start them indoors in a module tray or modest pot filled with peat-free seed compost, then pot on and move out in late spring or early summer once growing strongly. Outdoors, sow in light shade, leaving 12–18in (30–45cm) at first, and protect seedlings from slugs.

**Plant out:** Late spring or early summer (May–June) is best for bought or seed-raised plants, after hardening off. Light shade is ideal, though full sun is acceptable, on rich, moist soil that neither dries out nor lies wet.

**Spacing:** 12–18in (30–45cm) at planting, but an established plant can spread to about 3¼ft (1m) wide, with flower stalks to 6ft (2m). A pot should be at least 1ft (30cm) wide and deep, with good drainage holes.

**Care:** Little work once settled: no routine feed or weeding is usually needed. Water new plants regularly through their first season; like celery, it likes moisture but not waterlogging, and may need water in long droughts. If leafy growth stalls later in the season, shear off the tired foliage to prompt a second flush. Young stems can be blanched like trench celery, by wrapping a newspaper sleeve around stems about 1ft (30cm) tall, or by earthing up.

**Harvest window:** January–February and April–October on the calendar (a winter gap is typical as plants die back). Pick young leaves regularly in spring and summer for a sweeter, celery-like flavour with parsley and aniseed notes. Once flowering begins in late summer the leaves become harsh, so either stop picking or pinch out the flowering stem to keep the foliage usable. Young blanched stems can be eaten like celery.

**Storing:** Used fresh in the main; a long leaf store is not described.

**Common problems:** Celery leaf mining fly, slugs and snails.

**In the kitchen:** Lovage is a large, leafy perennial, up to about 6 feet (180 cm) tall and 30 inches (80 cm) or more across, with hollow celery-like stalks and glossy, dark-green, lacy leaves, later bearing yellow flowers in umbels. The young leaves can stand in for celery in salads, teas and cooked dishes, and are best used fresh. It is a Mediterranean and south-west Asian plant, used by the ancient Greeks and Romans, and later introduced to Britain.

**Varieties:**

- **Lovage** (recommended)

### Marjoram

**Sow:** Sweet and pot marjoram seed is widely available and is best sown under cover in spring (March–May); named cultivars may be harder to find as seed. Scatter the tiny seed on moist peat-free seed mix – no covering is needed – and pot on into 3in (7.5cm) pots once seedlings have several leaves.

**Plant out:** Late spring or summer (May–July), after hardening off, for seed-raised, bought or overwintered plants. Pots are best for sweet and pot marjoram, so they can come indoors in autumn, away from winter cold and wet. Use a peat-free soil-based compost with up to a quarter horticultural grit by volume for drainage.

**Spacing:** At least 8in (20cm) from neighbours, as clumps spread. One young plant to a pot at least 6in (15cm) wide and deep. Mature mounds are often about 12in (30cm) tall and wide, though some types reach 18–20in (45–50cm).

**Care:** Easy and drought-tolerant once settled, and not a hungry plant. Water new plantings in dry spells; established plants in the ground rarely need extra water, but pots dry quickly in heat. To keep plants from year to year, move sweet marjoram, and often pot marjoram, under cover for winter.

**Harvest window:** May–October. Snip a few shoots and strip the leaves; flavour is best before the flowers open. Flowers are edible too. Fresh growth in spring, summer and early autumn supplies leaves, and plants kept under cover may stay productive in winter.

**Storing:** Leaves are often used fresh but can also be dried for later.

**Common problems:** Mint rust, mint moth, sage and Ligurian leafhoppers.

**In the kitchen:** Marjoram is a small, branching sub-shrub, sometimes up to 3 feet (90 cm) tall, so similar to oregano that the two are often confused, with light-green, slightly oval leaves in pairs. Strip the leaves from the stem and use them in both savoury and sweet dishes. It is native to the eastern Mediterranean, growing wild on hillsides in Italy, Greece, Turkey and Egypt.

**Varieties:**

- **'French' AGM** (recommended)
- **Sweet marjoram AGM** (recommended)

### Mint

**Sow:** Usually increased by division or cuttings, which is cheap, but seed can be sown under cover in spring: scatter on damp peat-free seed mix, press lightly, do not cover, and germinate at about 68°F (20°C) in a propagator or under a clear bag. Pot on into 3in (7.5cm) pots once seedlings have several leaves.

**Plant out:** Bought plants are sold year-round and can go in at any time, but settle best in spring or autumn (calendar March–May and September–November). Sun or partial shade. Plant in a pot of peat-free multi-purpose or soil-based compost to stop the underground stems (rhizomes) invading the border; if you plant in a bed, contain the roots.

**Spacing:** Grown in a pot to restrain spread rather than at a stated in-ground spacing.

**Care:** Easy: pick young leaves often to keep plants bushy. Container-grown clumps should be split and given fresh compost every few years, and watered whenever the pot dries – keep the compost evenly moist, checking often in hot weather. When flowering finishes, cut the plant down to about 2in (5cm) from the base.

**Harvest window:** May–October, from late spring until stems die back in winter. Pick regularly, choosing the young, soft tips for the strongest flavour; the more you pick, the more they produce. For a winter supply, bring pots indoors in autumn onto a sunny sill.

**Storing:** Bringing a pot indoors is a reliable way to keep winter pickings coming. No other long store is described.

**Common problems:** Aphids, blue mint beetle, mint rust, mint moth, sage and Ligurian leafhoppers.

**In the kitchen:** Mint is an aromatic herb from 4 inches to 4 feet (10–120 cm) tall according to type, spreading by underground runners, with distinctive square stems and tiny purple, pink or white flowers. It is used fresh or dried in both sweet and savoury cooking, and is especially associated with Turkish, Indian, Vietnamese, Greek and Persian kitchens. It is native to the Mediterranean basin and is now naturalised across much of the temperate world.

**Varieties:**

- **Pineapple mint** (recommended)
- **Moroccan mint** (recommended)
- **Lime mint** (recommended)
- **Spearmint** (recommended)
- **Apple mint** (recommended)
- **Chocolate mint** (recommended)

### Oregano

**Sow:** Usually bought as plants, but seed of some species, including the native Origanum vulgare, is widely sold. Sow under cover in spring (March–May) on moist peat-free seed mix, without covering the tiny seed, and pot on into 3in (7.5cm) pots once seedlings have several leaves.

**Plant out:** Bought plants can go in at any time but settle quickly in late spring; seed-raised plants after the last frost in late spring or early summer (May–June), after hardening off. A warm, sunny spot, in well-drained soil or a pot.

**Spacing:** At least 8–12in (20–30cm) from neighbours. One young plant to a pot at least 6in (15cm) wide and deep. Plants usually reach 1–2ft (30–60cm) tall and wide, according to variety.

**Care:** Minimal work: pick young sprigs often for bushy growth, cut back faded flower stems, and do not let vigorous neighbours or fallen leaves smother the plants. Water new plantings in dry spells; established plants in the ground rarely need extra water, though they are drought-tolerant, pots still need occasional watering.

**Harvest window:** May–October, from late spring into autumn. Snip a few shoots and strip the leaves; flavour is best before the flowers open. Use fresh or dried.

**Storing:** Hang leafy sprigs in a dark, airy place for a few weeks, then strip the dry leaves and keep them in an airtight jar.

**Common problems:** Mint rust, mint moth, sage and Ligurian leafhoppers.

**In the kitchen:** Oregano is a branching, upright or creeping shrub, sometimes as tall as 3 feet (90 cm), with soft, fuzzy, arrow-shaped leaves in well-spaced pairs. Fresh or dried, it is a staple of Greek and Italian cooking. It is a traditional Mediterranean herb.

**Varieties:**

- **'Hot and Spicy' AGM** (recommended)

- **'Compactum' AGM** (recommended)

### Parsley

**Sow:** Easiest sown direct in the final spot, in the ground or in a pot, to avoid root disturbance (March–August). Seed can be slow, often taking a month or more, so be patient. For earlier pickings, sow under cover about ½in (1cm) deep in modules, and move plants out at 4–6in (10–15cm) tall, or keep one or two on a sunny kitchen sill. Outdoors, thinly in a ½in (1cm) drill, rows 8–12in (20–30cm) apart according to variety. In a pot at least 10in (25cm) wide and deep, use peat-free compost.

**Plant out:** Mid-spring to early summer (April–June) for bought plugs or pots, after hardening off, in full sun or partial shade, in a pot or in the ground. They can also stay on a well-lit indoor sill.

**Spacing:** Rows 8–12in (20–30cm) apart. A pot should be at least 10in (25cm) wide and deep. Plants stand 12–30in (30–60cm) tall according to variety.

**Care:** Little work once settled: water in dry weather, keep weeds down, and snip off yellowing lower leaves to encourage new growth. Keep the soil or compost moist but not waterlogged, watering young plants until they settle and in hot summer droughts. Pots dry quickly in warm weather and need close attention.

**Harvest window:** May–November, from late spring into autumn; regular picking encourages more leaves. Take a few outer leafy stems, snipped near the base. Outdoor plants die down in winter – bring them indoors, into a glasshouse or cold frame, or cover those in the ground, to keep a supply going.

**Storing:** Best used fresh, but leaves can be frozen (chopped in ice-cube trays topped up with water) or dried: suspend a handful of cut stems somewhere warm, dark and airy for a few weeks, then crush and keep in an airtight jar.

**Common problems:** Aphids, carrot fly, celery leaf mining fly, slugs and snails.

**Varieties:**

- **'Titan' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Moss Curled' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Bravour' AGM** (recommended)
- **'French'** (recommended)
- **'Envy' AGM** (recommended)
- **Italian parsley** (recommended) — Italian, or flat-leaf, parsley grows about a foot (30 cm) tall and wide, with bright-green stems that toughen with age and triangular, deeply toothed, three-part leaves. The stems are a classic part of a bouquet garni for stocks, soups and brines, and the leaves are used fresh in cooking. Like curly parsley it is a Mediterranean herb; Linnaeus pointed to Sardinia in particular.
- **Curly parsley** — Curly parsley is a slow, shrubby biennial, about 12 inches (30 cm) tall and wide, with light-green stems and thickly ruffled, dark-green triangular leaves; in its second year it may send up tiny yellow flowers. The lacy foliage is mostly used fresh as a garnish, though it can be dried. It is a southern European Mediterranean plant; Linnaeus thought it came from southern France and Sardinia.

### Rosemary

**Sow:** Seed is possible under cover in spring (March–May), but plants take several years to reach a useful size; bought plants or cuttings are much quicker. The main reason to use seed is to raise many plants cheaply.

**Plant out:** Spring (March–May) is best, once the soil is warming, though bought plants are sold year-round. Seed-raised plants can go out once at least 4in (10cm) tall, after hardening off. Full sun and light, free-draining soil – it enjoys the drainage of a pot, which should be at least 1ft (30cm) wide and deep, with plenty of drainage holes.

**Spacing:** At least 18in (45cm) between plants. Pot at least 1ft (30cm) wide and deep.

**Care:** Easy once settled in a warm, sunny, free-draining spot. Water new plants in dry spells through the first year; established plants in the ground rarely need extra water, though pots do in drought. Trim lightly every year after flowering to keep plants bushy, but do not cut back into old wood. Upright types can be kept as a neat hedge with annual trimming. Unpruned plants go leggy and bare at the base and rarely recover from hard pruning, so replace old, straggly specimens from cuttings.

**Harvest window:** Year-round, as it is evergreen, though tender current-season shoots from May onwards taste best. Take sprigs as you need them, shaping the bush as you go. Use fresh or dried.

**Storing:** Hang sprigs in a warm, dark, airy place; when fully dry, strip the leaves and keep them in an airtight jar.

**Common problems:** Frost damage, cuckoo spit (spittlebugs), rosemary beetle, sage and Ligurian leafhoppers, scale insects.

**In the kitchen:** Rosemary is a pine-scented shrub that can reach about 4 feet (1.2 m) in a favourable climate, with flat, inch-long (2.5 cm) needle leaves, green above and silver-grey beneath, packed in upright rows. It is a strong herb, best used sparingly, and pairs well with other Mediterranean seasonings in cooking. It is native from the Mediterranean to western Asia, especially along the coast, and is now naturalised in many temperate regions.

**Varieties:**

- **'Foxtail' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Rosea' AGM** (recommended)
- **'Miss Jessopp's Upright' AGM** (recommended)

### Sage

**Sow:** Common sage can be raised from seed under cover in spring (March–May), in moist peat-free seed mix barely covered with fine compost, in a propagator or under a bag on a warm sill, but plants take at least a year to reach picking size; bought plants or cuttings are much quicker.

**Plant out:** Spring (March–May) is best, once the soil is warming, though pot-grown plants are sold year-round. Indoor-raised plants should be at least 4in (10cm) tall and hardened off. Full sun and free-draining soil that does not lie wet. A pot should be at least 12in (30cm) wide and deep, with plenty of drainage holes.

**Spacing:** At least 12in (30cm) from neighbours. Pot at least 12in (30cm) wide and deep. Mature plants reach 1–3ft (30–90cm) tall and wide, according to variety.

**Care:** Easy once settled. Water new plants in dry spells through the first year; established plants in the ground are drought-tolerant and rarely need extra water, but check pots in summer and keep the compost slightly moist. Pick young leaves regularly for bushy growth. Lightly prune established plants every mid- to late spring; hard cutting into bare wood is risky, as old stems may not reshoot, so replace elderly, straggly plants. Trim faded flower stems in late summer.

**Harvest window:** Year-round, as it is evergreen, but fresh spring and summer growth has the best flavour. Pick whole young shoots or single leaves as needed. In winter, biodegradable fleece, or a sheltered spot for pots, helps keep leaves in good condition.

**Storing:** Best used fresh, but leaves can be dried and stored for later.

**Common problems:** Capsid bugs, powdery mildew, rosemary beetle, sage and Ligurian leafhoppers.

**In the kitchen:** Sage is a bushy herb with stems over 20 inches (50 cm) tall, bearing narrow, grey-green, felty leaves up to 2½ inches (6 cm) long and about ⅝ inch (1.5 cm) wide, with a silvery bloom. Use it sparingly, fresh or more often cooked, so it does not drown other flavours. It is native to the countries around the Mediterranean and has been recorded for more than 4,000 years.

**Varieties:**

- **'Icterina' AGM** (recommended)

### Savory

**Sow:** Start seed under cover in spring (March–May): sow thinly on the surface of moist peat-free seed mix, without covering, and germinate at about 68°F (20°C) in a propagator or under a clear bag on a bright sill. Seedlings usually appear in two to three weeks. Summer, winter and creeping types are grown.

**Plant out:** Spring (March–May), once the soil is warming, after hardening off seed-raised plants or cuttings. An open, sunny spot with free-draining soil that never lies wet; on heavy or damp ground use a raised bed or a pot. Plant at the same depth as before.

**Spacing:** At least 8in (20cm) from neighbours. A pot should be at least 8in (20cm) wide and deep, with good drainage holes. Summer savory makes a bush about 1ft (30cm) tall and wide; winter savory can reach 16in (40cm); creeping savory is about 4in (10cm) tall and 12in (30cm) across.

After flowering, trim summer savory for a flush of shoots into autumn. Remove faded flower stems of winter and creeping types unless you want seed. Lightly prune winter savory in mid- to late spring if it has grown straggly.

**Harvest window:** Year-round on the calendar, according to type. Use leafy shoots fresh for the best rich, spicy, peppery flavour. Seed-raised plants can be picked over lightly once they are about 4in (10cm) tall. Strip leaves from the stems and chop them finely. Leaves can also be dried.

**Storing:** Hang sprigs in a warm, dark, airy place to dry for later use.

**Common problems:** No particular problems are listed.

**Varieties:**

- **Summer savory** (recommended)
- **Winter savory** (recommended)

### Sorrel

**Sow:** French, common and red-veined types are easy from seed, under cover or outdoors (March–June), and will self-sow if allowed to flower. Under cover in spring, sow about ½in (1cm) deep in moist peat-free modules or small pots, in a warm bright place; seedlings usually appear in one to two weeks, then pot on into 3in (7.5cm) pots. In the open, from April into early summer, sow a short drill ½in (1cm) deep.

**Plant out:** Spring to early summer (March–June), after hardening off, in sun or partial shade. Most soils are tolerated, but leaf crops are better on rich, moisture-holding ground.

**Spacing:** Varies with type: French can form a mat 6–18in (15–45cm) tall and up to 3ft (1m) across; some upright types reach about 20in (50cm); clumps are often 16–24in (40–60cm) tall and about 12in (30cm) wide. Follow the packet for planting distances.

**Care:** Little work once settled. Snip out flower stems to keep plants leafing and to prevent self-sowing if you do not want more plants. Mulch each year and divide every four or five years to keep clumps productive. Water seedlings and new plants in dry spells until well rooted; established plants in the ground need less attention.

**Harvest window:** March–November. Young leaves from when growth starts in spring right into autumn. Regular picking keeps leaves young and tender, for salads, sandwiches, scattering over cooked dishes, or steaming like spinach. Remove old, bitter leaves to encourage a new flush.

**Storing:** Used fresh; no long store is described.

**Common problems:** Aphids, slugs and snails.

**Varieties:**

- **French sorrel** (recommended)
- **Red-veined sorrel** (recommended)
- **Common sorrel** (recommended) — Garden sorrel is a hardy, deep-rooted clump, 12–24 inches (30–60 cm) tall and as wide, with large, soft, light-green leaves from a central rosette, and later tall spikes of small reddish-green flowers. Young, tender leaves can be used raw as a salad green or herb, or cooked. It is a European and Asian plant, long cultivated, and now widely naturalised in damp temperate soils.

### Tarragon

**Sow:** French tarragon is rarely sold as seed; Russian tarragon comes easily from a pinch of seed in spring (March–June), indoors or in the open – one plant is usually enough. Indoors, just veil the seed with a dusting of fine compost in a small pot of damp peat-free seed mix, in a warm bright place, then move seedlings into 3in (7.5cm) pots once they have several leaves.

**Plant out:** Mid-spring to early summer (April–June), after hardening off. Both types like free-draining ground and a warm, sunny, sheltered spot. Russian tarragon can go in the ground or in a pot; French is best in a pot so it can be moved under cover in winter.

**Spacing:** Russian types at least 12in (30cm) from other plants (they can reach about 3½ft / 1m tall and 20in / 50cm wide); less vigorous French types 8in (20cm) apart (typically 20–30in / 50–75cm tall and about 12in / 30cm wide). One plant to a pot at least 8in (20cm) wide and deep.

**Care:** Little work once settled, especially for Russian types. French tarragon usually needs protection from wet UK winters. Pinch out stems with flower buds to prolong the leaf harvest. Renew the clump every few years by splitting or taking cuttings, because tarragon, especially French, is generally a short-lived perennial. Water new plants in dry spells through the first season.

**Harvest window:** March–November, from when new growth starts in spring into autumn. Pick shoot tips regularly for fresh young growth. Strip and finely chop the leaves to flavour salads, sauces, and dishes of mushrooms, fish, eggs or chicken. Best used fresh; dried leaves have a milder flavour.

**Storing:** Leaves can be dried and kept in airtight containers for winter use.

**Common problems:** Powdery mildew, rust diseases.

**In the kitchen:** Tarragon is a shrub-like herb with slender stems and many pairs of thin, semi-glossy, lance-shaped leaves typically ¾–3 inches (2–8 cm) long. It is used fresh or dried worldwide, but is most fragrant when added fresh at the end of cooking. Experts think it originated in Mongolia and Siberia, later spreading through Asia and Europe.

**Varieties:**

- **Russian tarragon** (recommended)
- **French tarragon** (recommended)

### Thyme

**Sow:** Several species are sold as seed. Seed-raised plants may take about a year to settle before you start picking, but seed is economical for edging or a thyme lawn. Sow under cover in spring (March–May) on moist peat-free seed mix, without covering the seed, and pot on into 3in (7.5cm) pots once seedlings have several leaves.

**Plant out:** Mid-spring to early summer (April–June) is best, after hardening off, in a warm, sunny, free-draining spot – herb gardens, dry gardens, low ground cover or a pot all suit it.

**Spacing:** 8–12in (20–30cm) apart according to variety, at the same depth as before. One plant to a pot at least 6in (15cm) wide and 4in (10cm) deep. Some form small mounds up to 1ft (30cm) tall.

**Care:** Little work once settled: a light trim after flowering keeps plants compact. After some years, shrubby types can grow straggly – take cuttings or layer them for replacements. Do not let vigorous neighbours or fallen leaves smother the plants. It shrugs off drought and, once rooted, usually needs water only as a new planting, or when container-grown in hot, dry weather. Give shrubby types a gentle clip in mid- to late spring and/or just after they bloom; cutting hard into aged woody stems is risky. Creeping types need only a very light trim.

**Harvest window:** Year-round, as it is evergreen, though tender current-season shoots from May onwards taste best. Take young sprigs as needed; regular picking keeps plants compact and encourages new growth. Flowers are flavourful too.

**Storing:** Fresh sprigs are the usual way to use it; a long dry-store method is not spelled out.

**Common problems:** Rosemary beetle, sage and Ligurian leafhoppers, slugs and snails.

**In the kitchen:** Thyme is a low herb, 6–12 inches (15–30 cm) tall and spreading up to 18 inches (45 cm), either upright, matting or cascading according to type, with tiny purple, pink or white flowers when mature. It can be used fresh or dried, and keeps its flavour unusually well when dried. It is a drought-tolerant Mediterranean plant, growing wild on the hills of Greece and in southern France, Italy and Spain.

**Varieties:**

- **'Snowdrift'** (recommended)
- **'Sparkling Bright'** (recommended)
- **'Lemon Curd'** (recommended)
- **Caraway thyme** (recommended)
- **Broad-leaved thyme** (recommended)
- **Common thyme** (recommended)
- **Lemon thyme** — Lemon thyme is a spreading, shrubby ground-cover, 6–12 inches (15–30 cm) tall and 18 inches (45 cm) or more across, with tiny leaves clustered on thin stems that become woody at the base. Use it fresh or dried, on its own or blended with other kitchen herbs. Like other thymes it is a Mediterranean plant, grown for thousands of years.

### Calendula (pot marigold)

*Calendula officinalis*

Calendula, or pot marigold, bears large composite flower heads, typically 2–3 inches (5–7 cm) across, of ray petals around a central disc. The petals have a mild, herbal, slightly tangy, bitter flavour and are used fresh or cooked once taken off the flower base. The plant is native around the Mediterranean — southern Europe, Egypt and parts of western Asia — and has grown wild since ancient times.

### Cape gooseberry (golden berry)

*Physalis peruviana*

Cape gooseberries, also sold as golden berries, are small fruits, typically ½–1¼ inches (1.25–3 cm) across and ⅛–⅓ oz (4–10 g), round to oval, with smooth, thin, glossy skin and a sweet-tart, tropical flavour, used fresh, cooked or dried once the papery husk is removed. They are native to the Andes of Peru, Chile, Ecuador and Colombia, and have been used there since ancient times.

### Elderberry

Elderberries are tiny, usually less than ⅜ inch (1 cm) across, round to oval, with delicate skin in purple-blue to almost black. The source is clear that they should not be eaten raw, as they are considered toxic until cooked; cooking them with a sweetener also softens their sharpness. Elder is an ancient plant of several regions, mostly north of the equator, with a handful of species in South America and Australia.

### Lavender

Lavender is a short, upright, evergreen or semi-evergreen shrub, varying by species, with woody branches and soft silver-green to grey-green foliage. Only certain kinds are used in the kitchen, where the flavour is fragrant, floral and sweet, in both fresh and cooked dishes. Its likely homeland is the Mediterranean, perhaps also parts of India and the Middle East, and it has grown wild since antiquity.

### Nasturtium

*Tropaeolum majus*

Nasturtium leaves are round, waxy, pliable, bright-green discs, typically 2–6 inches (5–15 cm) across, on a central stem, from a trailing vine that also bears colourful flowers. The foliage is mainly used raw, chopped into salads, for a spicy, peppery bite. Modern garden types descend from two Peruvian species, carried to Europe by Spanish conquistadors.

### Sea kale

*Crambe maritima*

Sea kale is a coastal perennial, forming clumps about 2 feet (60 cm) across and 2½ feet (75 cm) tall, with fleshy, silver-grey, wavy-edged leaves, scented four-petalled white flowers and pea-sized pods with a single edible seed. Young leaves, stems and pods can be eaten raw for a bittersweet note, or blanched, steamed, roasted and sautéed once they mature. It is native to western European shores and the Black Sea, first taken into gardens in the 1600s and widely planted on both sides of the Atlantic during the 19th century — Jefferson put it into the garden at Monticello in 1809.

### Upland cress

*Barbarea verna*

Upland cress forms a rosette about 6–8 inches (15–20 cm) across of glossy, rounded, lightly serrated dark-green leaves on thin, crisp stems. Use it raw in salads or steam, boil and sauté it, much as you would watercress. It is native to south-western Europe and has been grown in England since the 17th century, later naturalising in the United States.

---

### Watercress

*Nasturtium officinale*

Watercress is a small aquatic green: hollow, crisp stems typically 8–10 inches (20–25 cm) long, each bearing about three to nine round, scalloped leaflets. Use the leaves and stems raw as a garnish or in salads, or wilt and lightly sauté them. It is native to Europe and Asia, recorded more than 3,000 years ago in the ancient Mediterranean world, and later taken from the wild into cultivation.
